Impact of Spatial Audio on CPU Benchmarking Hardware

This article examines how spatial audio technologies like Dolby Atmos and Windows Sonic affect CPU performance during hardware benchmarking. It explains the mechanics of object-based audio processing, quantifies the actual CPU overhead across entry-level and high-end processors, and differentiates between the impacts on synthetic benchmarks versus real-world gaming tests. Finally, it provides actionable guidelines on configuring audio settings to ensure accurate and consistent benchmarking results.

How Spatial Audio Uses the CPU

Traditional surround sound sends audio to fixed speaker channels. Spatial audio, such as Dolby Atmos and Windows Sonic, uses object-based audio. Instead of fixed channels, sounds are treated as independent objects placed in a three-dimensional space.

Because most modern PCs lack dedicated sound cards with built-in Digital Signal Processors (DSPs), the central processor (CPU) must handle these spatial calculations. The CPU uses algorithms called Head-Related Transfer Functions (HRTF) to calculate how sound waves bounce and travel to the listener's ears in real-time. This requires active CPU cycles and thread allocation whenever audio is playing.

Impact on Synthetic CPU Benchmarks

Synthetic benchmarks like Cinebench, Geekbench, and Prime95 do not generate complex audio streams. During these tests, the impact of having Dolby Atmos or Windows Sonic enabled is practically zero.

  • Idle Overhead: The Windows Audio Device Graph Isolation process (audiodg.exe) manages spatial audio. When no audio is playing, this process consumes less than 0.1% of CPU resources.
  • Benchmark Scores: Enabling spatial audio without active playback will fall entirely within the standard margin of error for synthetic CPU tests.

Impact on Gaming and System Benchmarks

Benchmarks that include active audio— primarily built-in game benchmarks (e.g., Cyberpunk 2077, Shadow of the Tomb Raider)—show a measurable CPU performance hit when spatial audio is enabled. Games often generate anywhere from 32 to over 100 simultaneous audio objects that the CPU must track and render in 3D space.

The performance impact depends heavily on the hardware being tested:

  • High-End CPUs (12+ Cores): Processors like the Intel Core i9 or AMD Ryzen 9 series have enough spare threads to handle HRTF calculations without bottlenecking the graphics card. The average frame rate (FPS) drop is typically between 0.5% and 2%.
  • Mid-Range to Low-End CPUs (4 to 6 Cores): When a CPU is already running near 100% utilization to process game logic and physics, adding spatial audio creates thread contention. This can result in a 3% to 8% drop in average FPS.
  • 1% Low Frame Rates: The most noticeable impact during benchmarking is on 1% and 0.1% low frame rates. Heavy audio scenes (such as explosions with multiple debris sounds) spike CPU usage, leading to micro-stuttering and lower minimum frame rate scores.

Dolby Atmos vs. Windows Sonic Overhead

While both technologies use the Microsoft Spatial Sound API, they process audio differently and have slightly different hardware footprints.

  • Windows Sonic: Built directly into Windows, Sonic is optimized for low overhead. It uses slightly less CPU power, making it less intrusive during heavy workloads.
  • Dolby Atmos for Headphones: Atmos generally utilizes more complex acoustic modeling. In CPU-bound gaming benchmarks, Dolby Atmos can consume 1% to 2% more CPU resources than Windows Sonic, translating to a marginally lower benchmark score on thread-limited systems.

Best Practices for Hardware Benchmarking

To maintain integrity when benchmarking CPU hardware, spatial audio settings must be strictly controlled.

  1. Standardize Audio Settings: Always use standard stereo (16-bit or 24-bit, 48000 Hz) with all spatial audio and "enhancements" disabled when testing raw CPU performance. This isolates the hardware from software-induced audio bottlenecks.
  2. Keep Audio Devices Consistent: Do not switch between USB headsets (which may use their own software drivers) and 3.5mm jacks (which use motherboard Realtek drivers) mid-test. Different drivers utilize the CPU differently.
  3. Test Spatial Audio Separately: If the goal is to benchmark how a CPU handles a true consumer gaming load, turn on Dolby Atmos or Windows Sonic, but ensure it remains enabled across every CPU being compared in that specific test suite.
