Why Reviewers Use Contact Frames for Thermal Benchmarking

Hardware reviewers frequently use aftermarket CPU contact frames on Intel LGA1700, LGA1851, and AMD AM5 sockets to ensure accurate, consistent, and repeatable thermal benchmarking results. By replacing the factory retention mechanisms on motherboards, testers eliminate the physical warping of processors, standardize mounting pressure across multiple tests, and simplify the maintenance required when constantly swapping out cooling solutions. Ultimately, these frames remove mechanical flaws as a variable, allowing reviewers to accurately measure the true performance of a CPU cooler.

Preventing CPU Bending on LGA1700 and LGA1851

The primary reason contact frames gained popularity begins with the physical design of Intel’s LGA1700 and LGA1851 processors. Unlike older, square-shaped CPUs, these modern Intel processors are rectangular and feature a thinner substrate.

The stock Intel retention bracket—known as the Independent Loading Mechanism (ILM)—applies immense downward force onto just two central points on the long edges of the CPU. Because the pressure is not distributed evenly, the CPU's Integrated Heat Spreader (IHS) bows and warps inward.

When the surface of the CPU becomes concave, the flat base of a CPU cooler cannot make full contact with the center of the processor, which is right where the heat-generating cores are located. Custom contact frames replace the stock ILM and apply even pressure around the entire perimeter of the CPU. This keeps the metal surface completely flat, resulting in optimal contact and dropping temperatures by anywhere from 3 to 10 degrees Celsius.

Ensuring Benchmark Consistency and Repeatability

For a hardware reviewer, data consistency is more important than raw temperature drops. A reviewer might mount and dismount fifty different coolers on the same CPU over a year.

With a stock Intel bracket, the CPU can warp further over time with repeated thermal cycles and cooler mounts. This means a cooler tested in January might perform better than an identical cooler tested in July simply because the CPU became more bent over those six months, ruining the integrity of the benchmark database. A rigid contact frame locks the CPU into a permanently flat state, ensuring the physical contact area remains identical from the first test to the hundredth.

Simplifying Maintenance on AMD AM5

AMD’s AM5 socket uses a square processor with a very thick heat spreader, meaning it does not suffer from the severe bending issues seen on Intel platforms. While AM5 contact frames do ensure even mounting pressure, reviewers use them for a much more practical reason: cleaning thermal paste.

To make room for surface-mounted capacitors, AMD designed the AM5 heat spreader with unique cutouts around the edges, often referred to as an "octopus" shape. When a cooler is mounted, excess thermal paste is squeezed directly into these crevices and onto the delicate components below.

For a regular user who builds a PC once, this is a minor annoyance. For a reviewer who has to apply and wipe away thermal paste multiple times a day, it is a massive time sink. Custom contact frames for AM5 are designed with precise inner edges or silicone gaskets that seal off these cutouts. Reviewers can quickly wipe the CPU clean with a single pass of a cloth, speeding up the testing workflow significantly.

Removing the Socket as a Bottleneck

The goal of thermal benchmarking is to test the limits of the cooling hardware, not the flaws of the motherboard socket. High-end liquid coolers and dual-tower air coolers are engineered with specific cold plate curvatures to maximize heat transfer. If a stock motherboard bracket deforms the CPU, it creates a thermal bottleneck. By using a contact frame, reviewers create an idealized, standardized testing environment that allows coolers to perform at their maximum engineered potential.
