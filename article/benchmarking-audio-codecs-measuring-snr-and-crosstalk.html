Benchmarking Audio Codecs: Measuring SNR and Crosstalk

This article explains the exact methodologies and tools used to benchmark computer hardware audio codecs, specifically focusing on Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and stereo crosstalk. It covers the physical test setups, such as loopback configurations and dedicated audio analyzers, the software suites involved, and the step-by-step signal processing techniques required to capture accurate decibel readings for both metrics under various system conditions.

The Benchmarking Setup

To measure an onboard motherboard audio codec or a dedicated sound card, reviewers must capture the analog output of the device. This is achieved using one of two physical setups:

  1. External Audio Analyzer (Preferred): The Line-Out port of the computer is connected to a professional-grade external audio interface or a dedicated hardware analyzer (such as an Audio Precision APx555). This isolates the performance of the computer's Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC) because the measuring device has a noise floor far lower than the hardware being tested.
  2. Loopback Configuration: A shielded 3.5mm TRS cable connects the computer's Line-Out port directly to its own Line-In port. While cost-effective, this method measures the combined degradation of both the DAC (output) and the Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) (input), resulting in slightly worse scores than the output hardware produces on its own.

For software-based testing, RightMark Audio Analyzer (RMAA) is the standard tool used to generate the test tones and calculate the results.

Measuring Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)

Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) compares the level of a desired audio signal to the level of background noise (hiss, hum, and electrical interference) generated by the hardware. The measurement process follows three strict steps:

  • Step 1: Reference Signal Generation: The benchmarking software generates a pure, maximum-amplitude sine wave, typically at 1 kHz and 0 dBFS (decibels relative to full scale). The receiving analyzer measures the Root Mean Square (RMS) voltage of this output to establish the maximum signal ceiling.
  • Step 2: Noise Floor Capture: The software stops the 1 kHz tone and plays a "digital zero" signal (a continuous stream of 0-bit data, or a maximally quiet dithered signal). A digital zero signal is used instead of simply stopping playback because many modern codecs automatically shut off their amplifiers when no signal is detected. This "auto-mute" feature would artificially inflate the SNR score.
  • Step 3: Weighting and Calculation: The analyzer measures the residual electrical noise present during the silent signal. An "A-weighting" filter is typically applied to this measurement. A-weighting shapes the measurement to match human hearing sensitivity, ignoring extreme high and low frequencies that humans cannot hear. The difference between the 1 kHz reference signal and the residual noise floor is calculated and expressed in decibels (dB or dBA). Higher positive numbers indicate cleaner audio.

Measuring Crosstalk (Stereo Separation)

Crosstalk occurs when an audio signal transmitted on one stereo channel bleeds into the other channel, narrowing the stereo soundstage. In computer hardware, this is usually caused by closely packed PCB traces, shared ground planes, or poor capacitor placement on the motherboard.

  • Step 1: Channel Isolation: The testing software sends a 1 kHz full-scale sine wave (or a 20 Hz to 20 kHz frequency sweep) exclusively to the Left channel. Simultaneously, the Right channel is fed a silent digital zero signal.
  • Step 2: Leakage Detection: The analyzer ignores the active Left channel and measures the RMS voltage of the silent Right channel. It specifically looks for the presence of the 1 kHz tone that has electromagnetically leaked across the motherboard or sound card circuitry.
  • Step 3: Reversal and Calculation: The amplitude of the leaked signal in the silent channel is compared to the amplitude of the source signal in the active channel. The test is then reversed—playing the tone in the Right channel and measuring the Left—to ensure consistent PCB design. Crosstalk is expressed as a negative decibel value (e.g., -95 dB). Lower negative numbers (further from zero) indicate better channel separation.

Accounting for PC System Load

Unlike standalone audio equipment, computer audio codecs operate inside an environment heavy with Electromagnetic Interference (EMI). To get a true benchmark of PC hardware, both SNR and crosstalk tests are executed twice:

  1. System Idle: The computer sits at the desktop with minimal background processes.
  2. System Under Load: Stress-testing software (such as Prime95 and FurMark) is run simultaneously to maximize CPU and GPU power draw.

High electrical currents running through the motherboard and graphics card generate EMI, which can bleed into poorly shielded audio pathways. Running the SNR and crosstalk tests under load reveals how effectively the hardware manufacturer isolated the audio codec from the rest of the computer's components.
