DRAM Temperature and Stability at Tight tREFI Timings

This article explores the critical relationship between DRAM temperature and system stability when tuning the Refresh Interval (tREFI) for computer hardware benchmarking. It explains how memory cells store data, why elevated temperatures accelerate charge leakage, and how pushing tREFI to its limits under high heat leads to system crashes. Finally, it provides practical solutions for maintaining thermal stability during intensive benchmark runs.

Understanding tREFI

DRAM stores data as electrical charges inside millions of tiny capacitors. Because these capacitors naturally leak their charge over time, the memory controller must periodically pause operations to recharge, or "refresh," them.

The tREFI (Refresh Interval) timing dictates how much time passes between these refresh cycles. Unlike primary memory timings where lower numbers equal better performance, optimizing (or "tightening") tREFI means increasing the value. A higher tREFI value delays the refresh cycle, allowing the RAM to spend more time transferring data and less time refreshing. This significantly boosts memory bandwidth and lowers latency in hardware benchmarks.

How Temperature Affects Memory Cells

The physical enemy of a DRAM capacitor is heat. As the operating temperature of a memory module increases, the rate at which its capacitors leak electrical charge accelerates exponentially.

At standard operating temperatures (around 30°C to 40°C), capacitors hold their charge effortlessly between standard refresh intervals. However, once temperatures climb past 50°C, the silicon becomes much more susceptible to leakage, and the stored electrical charges drain away significantly faster.

Why High Temperatures Crash Tuned tREFI

When benchmarking hardware, enthusiasts often push tREFI to its maximum selectable limit (such as 65,535 on DDR4 and up to 262,143 on some DDR5 motherboards). This stretches the time between refreshes to the absolute maximum. If the DRAM temperature rises too high during a heavy benchmark load, a critical conflict occurs: the capacitors leak their charge faster than the delayed refresh cycle can replenish it.

When a capacitor loses too much charge before the next refresh arrives, the memory controller can no longer read the data accurately. A "bit flip" occurs, turning a 1 into a 0. During a benchmark, this results in immediate instability. Symptoms include failed benchmark runs, application crashes, Blue Screens of Death (BSOD), and silent operating system file corruption.

Critical Temperature Thresholds

Different memory integrated circuits (ICs) have varying tolerances to heat. Popular overclocking ICs like Samsung B-die (DDR4) and SK Hynix A-die or M-die (DDR5) are notoriously sensitive to temperature when tREFI is maxed out. When running heavily tuned tREFI timings alongside elevated voltages, the general thermal limits are:

  • Under 45°C: Ideal for extreme tREFI tuning and maximum stability.
  • 45°C to 50°C: The borderline zone where instability begins to manifest during heavy, prolonged stress tests.
  • Over 50°C: High probability of memory errors and crashes when using aggressively tuned tREFI values.

Managing Thermals for Benchmarking

To maintain stability at tight, high-performance tREFI settings, benchmarkers must actively manage DRAM temperatures. This is especially true for DDR5, which places the heat-generating power management IC (PMIC) directly onto the memory stick, causing it to run hotter than DDR4.

To prevent temperature-induced tREFI instability, apply the following methods:

  • Use Active Cooling: Place a dedicated fan directly over the memory modules. Even a basic 120mm fan blowing directly across the DIMMs can drop temperatures by 10°C to 15°C, instantly stabilizing a maxed-out tREFI setting.
  • Account for GPU Heat: During 3D benchmarks, graphics cards exhaust massive amounts of heat directly into the path of the RAM slots. Always test your RAM stability while the GPU is under load to ensure ambient case heat does not push the DRAM over its thermal cliff.
  • Improve Heatspreader Contact: Factory heatspreaders sometimes use low-quality thermal pads that trap heat. Replacing stock thermal pads with high-conductivity pads, or running bare PCB sticks with a high-RPM fan, often yields much lower temperatures.
  • Dial Back tREFI: If adding active cooling is not an option, lower the tREFI value. Stepping down from 65,535 to 32,767 cuts the refresh delay in half, providing massive thermal headroom at the cost of a very minor drop in benchmark scores.
