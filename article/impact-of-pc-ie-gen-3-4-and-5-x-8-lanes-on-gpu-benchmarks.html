Impact of PCIe Gen 3, 4, and 5 x8 Lanes on GPU Benchmarks

This article examines how restricting a graphics card to an x8 PCIe lane configuration across PCIe Generation 3, Generation 4, and Generation 5 impacts GPU benchmarking and overall hardware performance. We will cover the raw bandwidth differences between each generation, explain why modern GPUs frequently operate at x8 speeds, and break down how these limitations affect both synthetic tests and real-world gaming benchmarks, particularly when Video RAM (VRAM) limits are reached.

The Bandwidth Breakdown

PCIe (Peripheral Component Interconnect Express) generations double the bandwidth per lane with each new iteration. When a graphics card is wired for or restricted to 8 lanes (x8) instead of the standard 16 lanes (x16), the available data transfer speeds are cut in half for that specific generation.

Here is the maximum theoretical bidirectional bandwidth for x8 configurations:

  • PCIe Gen 3 x8: ~8 GB/s (Equivalent to PCIe Gen 2 x16)
  • PCIe Gen 4 x8: ~16 GB/s (Equivalent to PCIe Gen 3 x16)
  • PCIe Gen 5 x8: ~32 GB/s (Equivalent to PCIe Gen 4 x16)

Because bandwidth doubles with each generation, a PCIe Gen 4 x8 connection offers the exact same bandwidth as a full PCIe Gen 3 x16 slot. Similarly, a PCIe Gen 5 x8 connection matches a full PCIe Gen 4 x16 slot.

Why GPUs Run at x8 Speeds

In computer hardware benchmarking, you will encounter x8 lane restrictions in two ways:

  1. Physical GPU Wiring: Manufacturers often wire budget and mid-range GPUs (such as the NVIDIA RTX 4060 or AMD RX 7600) with only 8 electrical lanes to reduce manufacturing costs, even though the physical connector is x16 in size.
  2. Motherboard Lane Bifurcation: On many modern motherboards, installing an M.2 NVMe SSD into a secondary slot that shares bandwidth with the CPU will force the primary x16 GPU slot to drop down to x8 mode.

Impact on Synthetic Benchmarks

Synthetic benchmarks test specific hardware limits and react differently depending on what is being measured.

  • Bandwidth-Specific Tests: In tests like the 3DMark PCI Express Feature Test, which purely measures how fast data moves across the PCIe bus, benchmark scores scale linearly. Gen 4 x8 will score roughly double that of Gen 3 x8, and Gen 5 x8 will score quadruple that of Gen 3 x8.
  • Standard Rendering Tests: In benchmarks like 3DMark Time Spy, Fire Strike, or Unigine Superposition, the necessary graphical assets are loaded entirely into the GPU's VRAM before the test begins. Because the PCIe bus is not heavily utilized during the actual rendering phase, the score difference between Gen 3 x8, Gen 4 x8, and Gen 5 x8 is negligible, typically showing only a 1% to 3% variance.

Impact on Gaming Benchmarks

Real-world gaming benchmarks rely heavily on streaming assets (textures, geometry, and shaders) from the system RAM to the GPU in real-time. The impact of x8 wiring here depends heavily on the GPU's VRAM capacity.

Scenario A: Sufficient VRAM

If the GPU has enough VRAM to hold all the game's data (for example, a 12GB or 16GB GPU running at 1440p), the PCIe bus is not stressed.

  • Gen 3 x8: Suffers a minor 2% to 5% drop in average frame rates compared to Gen 4 x8.
  • Gen 4 x8 and Gen 5 x8: Perform nearly identically, as 16 GB/s is more than enough bandwidth to feed the GPU without delay.

Scenario B: Exceeding VRAM Limits

If a game demands more VRAM than the GPU possesses (which is common on 8GB cards running modern titles at high settings), the GPU must use the PCIe bus to offload overflow data to the slower system RAM.

  • Gen 3 x8: The ~8 GB/s bandwidth becomes a massive bottleneck. Benchmarks will show severe frame time spikes, stuttering, and a 15% to 30% drop in both average FPS and 1% low frame rates.
  • Gen 4 x8: The ~16 GB/s bandwidth mitigates much of this penalty, resulting in significantly smoother 1% lows and higher averages compared to Gen 3 x8, though stuttering may still occur due to system RAM latency.
  • Gen 5 x8: The ~32 GB/s bandwidth completely removes the PCIe bus as a bottleneck during VRAM overflow, operating at the same efficiency as a standard Gen 4 x16 slot.

A Note on High-End GPUs and PCIe Gen 5

PCIe connections are backward and forward-compatible, but they always operate at the speed of the slowest component. If you place a current high-end PCIe Gen 4 GPU (like an RTX 4090) into a PCIe Gen 5 motherboard, and that motherboard drops to x8 mode due to M.2 SSD lane sharing, the GPU will not run at Gen 5 x8. Because the GPU only supports Gen 4, the connection defaults to Gen 4 x8. In benchmarks, this restricts a flagship GPU to 16 GB/s of bandwidth, resulting in a 2% to 6% performance loss depending on the game and resolution.

To actually benchmark at Gen 5 x8 speeds (32 GB/s), both the motherboard/CPU and the graphics card must natively support the PCIe 5.0 standard. When using true Gen 5 hardware, dropping to x8 lanes yields zero performance loss in benchmarks compared to Gen 4 x16.
