How FurMark Exposes VRM Over-Current Protection Trips

This article explores how FurMark’s signature "furry donut" rendering acts as an extreme stress test to expose Voltage Regulator Module (VRM) Over-Current Protection (OCP) limits in graphics cards. It covers the mechanics of the FurMark workload, how VRMs manage electrical current, why this specific rendering causes massive power spikes, and what an OCP trip reveals about the stability and design of computer hardware.

The Mechanics of the FurMark "Donut"

In standard gaming benchmarks, a graphics processing unit (GPU) experiences fluctuating workloads. One moment it is rendering complex geometry, and the next it is rendering a simple skybox. This variance gives the hardware brief micro-seconds to cool down and stabilize power draw.

FurMark eliminates these breaks. Its rotating "furry donut" (a torus covered in thousands of rendered hair strands) utilizes an aggressive OpenGL pixel shader workload combined with massive overdraw. Overdraw means the GPU is calculating and rendering multiple overlapping layers of pixels in the exact same spot before displaying the final frame.

This specific rendering technique forces nearly every execution unit, shader core, and memory controller on the GPU to operate at 100% capacity simultaneously and continuously. Because the workload never lets up, the GPU demands absolute maximum electrical current from the moment the test begins.

The Role of the VRM and OCP

To understand how FurMark breaks hardware stability, you must understand how a GPU receives power:

  • The VRM (Voltage Regulator Module): The power supply unit (PSU) sends 12-volt power to the graphics card. The GPU core, however, operates at roughly 1 volt. The VRM steps down this 12V input to 1V. Because of the laws of physics (Power = Voltage × Current), dropping the voltage by a factor of 12 increases the electrical current (amperage) by a factor of 12. A GPU pulling 300 watts at 1 volt is forcing the VRM to handle 300 amps of current.
  • OCP (Over-Current Protection): OCP is a vital safety circuit built into the VRM controller and the power supply. If the amperage flowing through the VRM exceeds the safe physical limits of the components (MOSFETs and chokes), OCP instantly cuts power to prevent the silicon from melting or catching fire.

How the Donut Exposes OCP Flaws

When a benchmark tester runs FurMark, the rendering of the donut interacts with the VRM and OCP in three distinct ways to expose hardware weaknesses:

1. Sustained Peak Amperage

Because the fur shader saturates the entire GPU core without pause, the current draw hits a ceiling far higher than any normal game could produce. If a graphics card manufacturer used cheap VRM components or set the OCP threshold too low to save costs, the sheer volume of sustained amperage demanded by the donut will trip the OCP immediately.

2. Thermal Runaway in the VRM

As the FurMark donut spins, the VRM works overtime and generates intense heat. Silicon components become less efficient as they get hotter. As the VRM heats up, its internal resistance changes, requiring it to pull even more current to deliver the same amount of power to the GPU. This creates a thermal runaway loop. Within minutes, the rising heat drives the current past the safety limit, triggering an OCP shutdown. This exposes inadequate VRM cooling (such as missing thermal pads) on the graphics card.

3. Initial Transient Spikes

The exact moment the FurMark rendering window opens, the GPU transitions from an idle state (drawing around 15 watts) to an absolute maximum load state (drawing hundreds of watts) in milliseconds. This sudden demand creates a massive transient power spike. If the VRM controller cannot regulate this instantaneous surge of current fast enough, the spike will breach the OCP ceiling and crash the system instantly.

Identifying an OCP Trip

When the FurMark donut successfully forces an OCP trip during benchmarking, it does not result in a standard software crash or a Blue Screen of Death (BSOD). Instead, hardware testers look for a specific behavior:

  • The computer screen instantly goes black.
  • The system shuts off completely, as if unplugged from the wall.
  • GPU or case fans may spin at 100% speed while the screen remains blank.
  • The PC will not turn back on until the power supply switch is flipped off for several seconds to discharge the tripped protection circuit.

By using the FurMark donut to intentionally provoke this reaction, hardware reviewers and overclockers can effectively identify defective graphics cards, under-engineered VRMs, and failing power supplies that would otherwise remain hidden until a critical moment in a real-world application.
