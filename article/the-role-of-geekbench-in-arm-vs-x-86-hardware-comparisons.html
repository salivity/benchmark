The Role of Geekbench in ARM vs x86 Hardware Comparisons

This article explores the critical role Geekbench plays in benchmarking and comparing ARM and x86 computer hardware architectures. As the computing landscape evolves with ARM processors challenging traditional x86 dominance across mobile, desktop, and server environments, having a standardized, cross-platform testing tool is essential. Below, we examine how Geekbench bridges the gap between different instruction sets, utilizes real-world workloads to measure performance, separates single-core and multi-core capabilities, and presents specific limitations that users must consider when evaluating hardware.

Bridging the Architectural Divide

Comparing ARM and x86 processors is inherently difficult because they use fundamentally different instruction set architectures (ISAs). x86 relies on Complex Instruction Set Computing (CISC), while ARM utilizes Reduced Instruction Set Computing (RISC). Because they process instructions differently, you cannot simply compare clock speeds (GHz) or core counts to determine which processor is faster.

Geekbench solves this problem by acting as a translator and equalizer. It is compiled natively for both ARM and x86 across multiple operating systems, including Windows, macOS, Linux, iOS, and Android. By running native code tailored to each architecture while executing the exact same mathematical and computational tasks, Geekbench provides a baseline score that can be directly compared regardless of the underlying hardware design.

Focus on Real-World Workloads

Older benchmarks often focused purely on raw theoretical compute power, such as floating-point operations per second (FLOPS). This approach often favors one architecture over another based on specific hardware optimizations that do not translate to everyday use.

Geekbench plays a vital role in modern comparisons by utilizing short, real-world workloads instead of purely synthetic stress tests. The benchmark runs a series of everyday tasks, including:

  • File compression and decompression
  • Image rendering and photo filtering
  • PDF rendering and HTML5 parsing
  • Machine learning and computer vision tasks
  • Navigation and ray tracing calculations

By measuring how quickly an ARM chip and an x86 chip complete these identical tasks, Geekbench offers a practical look at how the hardware will perform for the average user or developer.

Evaluating Single-Core and Multi-Core Efficiency

ARM and x86 processors often take different approaches to core configuration. For example, many modern ARM chips (like Apple Silicon) use a hybrid design of high-performance and high-efficiency cores, whereas traditional x86 chips historically relied on uniform cores with simultaneous multithreading (though x86 has recently adopted hybrid designs as well).

Geekbench separates its testing into two distinct categories:

  1. Single-Core Score: This measures the performance of a single main processor core. It is crucial for comparing the raw architectural efficiency and instructions per clock (IPC) of an ARM core against an x86 core. It predicts how responsive a system will feel during basic, non-parallel tasks.
  2. Multi-Core Score: This tests all available cores working simultaneously. It highlights how well an architecture scales under heavy workloads like video editing or 3D rendering, showing how effectively an ARM chip's core cluster competes against an x86 chip's thread count.

Baseline Calibration

To make scores easily digestible, Geekbench uses a baseline system. A specific x86 processor (for example, a mainstream Intel Core i7) is chosen as the baseline and given a score of 1,000 or 2,500, depending on the Geekbench version. Every other processor, whether ARM or x86, is scored relative to this baseline. A score that is double the baseline indicates double the performance in Geekbench's specific test suite. This removes the complexity of analyzing individual task completion times and gives consumers and engineers an immediate sense of hierarchy.

Limitations in Cross-Platform Comparisons

While Geekbench is the industry standard for quick cross-platform comparisons, its role must be contextualized by its limitations:

  • Burst Performance vs. Sustained Loads: Geekbench runs very brief tests with pauses in between. This prevents processors from overheating. Consequently, it measures peak "burst" performance. It often favors fanless ARM laptops or smartphones that might thermally throttle during a 30-minute sustained workload, whereas an actively cooled x86 desktop would maintain its speed.
  • Operating System Overhead: Because ARM and x86 chips often run on different operating systems (e.g., macOS vs. Windows, or iOS vs. Android), the benchmark is also inadvertently testing the efficiency of the OS scheduler and the compiler used to build the Geekbench app for that specific platform.
  • Lack of GPU/NPU Context in CPU Scores: While Geekbench has a "Compute" benchmark for GPUs, its primary CPU test does not account for specialized hardware encoders (like media engines) that ARM chips heavily rely on to beat x86 chips in specific tasks like video export.

Geekbench serves as the premier starting point for ARM vs. x86 comparisons by offering a fair, accessible, and standardized metric for burst CPU performance, though it must be paired with sustained workload testing for a complete hardware evaluation.
