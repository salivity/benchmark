Variable Rate Shading: Visual Quality vs. Benchmark Scores

This article explores Variable Rate Shading (VRS) and its dual impact on computer hardware benchmarking. It explains the core mechanics of VRS, how the technology inflates benchmark scores by reducing GPU workloads, and the resulting trade-offs in visual fidelity. By the end, you will understand how different VRS configurations shift the balance between raw performance metrics and on-screen image quality during hardware evaluations.

What is Variable Rate Shading?

Traditionally, a graphics processing unit (GPU) applies shading to every single pixel on the screen equally, known as 1x1 shading. Variable Rate Shading changes this by allowing the GPU to apply a single shading operation to a block of pixels—such as 1x2, 2x2, or even 4x4—and then broadcast that result to the entire group.

Developers and benchmarking tools use VRS to lower the shading rate in areas of the screen where the human eye is less likely to notice a drop in detail. These areas include objects in fast motion, deep shadows, smoke, or the far edges of the player's peripheral vision.

How VRS Boosts Benchmark Scores

In hardware benchmarking, the primary goal is to measure how many frames per second (FPS) a system can render, which translates into an overall performance score. VRS directly increases these scores by drastically cutting down the number of pixel shader operations the GPU must perform per frame.

When a benchmark enables VRS:

  • GPU Workload Decreases: Shading a 2x2 block of pixels requires only 25% of the compute power needed to shade those four pixels individually.
  • Frame Rates Increase: Because the GPU spends less time shading low-priority areas, it renders frames much faster.
  • Higher Final Scores: Faster frame times directly result in higher benchmark scores, often yielding performance gains between 10% and 50% depending on how aggressively VRS is applied.

The Cost to Visual Quality

While benchmark scores climb with VRS enabled, visual quality moves in the opposite direction. The degree of visual degradation depends entirely on the shading rate size used during the test.

  • Fine-Grained VRS (1x2 or 2x1): The impact on visual quality is nearly imperceptible in motion. You must pause the benchmark and zoom in to see minor softening on textures.
  • Coarse-Grained VRS (2x2): Noticeable artifacts begin to appear. Distant textures look slightly blurry, and fine details like grates, foliage, or text may exhibit aliasing (jagged edges).
  • Aggressive VRS (2x4 or 4x4): Visual quality drops severely. The screen displays obvious pixelation and a blocky "checkerboard" effect in areas where the algorithm decides detail is not needed.

Balancing Score and Quality in Hardware Testing

In the context of benchmarking computer hardware, VRS presents a specific challenge: a higher score no longer guarantees a better-looking image, only a faster one.

Synthetic benchmarks like 3DMark include dedicated VRS tests to measure a GPU's efficiency with the technology. These tests usually run in two passes: one without VRS to establish a baseline, and one with VRS enabled to measure the performance delta.

When evaluating hardware outside of dedicated VRS tests, reviewers must keep VRS settings identical across all tested GPUs. Comparing a graphics card running aggressive VRS to a card running native 1x1 shading invalidates the benchmark. The VRS-enabled card will post a vastly superior score, but it achieves that score by rendering a visually inferior image. For accurate hardware comparisons, VRS must either be disabled entirely to test raw rasterization power, or locked to a specific quality preset to ensure every GPU is doing the exact same amount of visual work.
