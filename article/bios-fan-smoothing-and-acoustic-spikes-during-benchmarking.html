BIOS Fan Smoothing and Acoustic Spikes During Benchmarking

This article explores the relationship between motherboard BIOS fan smoothing time delays and acoustic performance during computer hardware benchmarking. It explains why sudden temperature surges cause jarring fan noise and how configuring step-up and step-down delays in the BIOS stabilizes fan speeds, eliminating disruptive acoustic spikes without compromising thermal safety.

The Cause of Acoustic Spikes During Benchmarking

Modern processors and graphics cards boost their clock speeds and voltages within milliseconds when a benchmark begins. This instantaneous power draw causes an immediate surge in core temperatures.

By default, motherboard fan curves react instantly to these temperature readings. When a CPU jumps from 40°C to 80°C in a single second, the motherboard commands the cooling fans to jump from idle speeds to maximum RPM immediately. This sudden acceleration creates a sharp, loud acoustic spike. Because many benchmarking suites cycle through various tests with brief pauses or loading screens in between, the fans constantly rev up and down, creating a distracting and jarring noise profile.

How BIOS Fan Smoothing Works

Fan smoothing—often labeled in the BIOS as Fan Step-Up/Step-Down Time, Fan Hysteresis, or Temperature Delay—introduces a time buffer between a thermal sensor reading and the fan's physical response.

Instead of reacting to the exact temperature at any given millisecond, the BIOS averages the temperature over a user-defined period (usually measured in seconds) or requires the temperature to remain at a specific threshold for that duration before changing the fan speed.

There are two primary controls for this feature:

  • Step-Up Delay: Dictates how slowly the fan accelerates when temperatures rise.
  • Step-Down Delay: Dictates how slowly the fan decelerates after temperatures drop.

The Impact on Benchmarking Acoustics

Applying a fan smoothing time delay directly alters the acoustic behavior of a PC under synthetic loads in three ways:

1. Elimination of False-Alarm Revving

Before a benchmark fully loads, background processes and test initialization cause brief, one-to-two-second CPU load spikes. Without a time delay, fans loudly rev up and immediately spin down. A step-up delay of two to three seconds forces the fans to ignore these momentary surges, allowing the physical mass of the CPU cooler's heatsink or liquid loop to absorb the brief heat spike silently.

2. Gradual Acoustic Transitions

When a sustained benchmark actually begins, a time delay prevents the jarring acoustic wall of fans instantly hitting 100% speed. Instead, the BIOS forces the fans to ramp up gradually over the set delay period. A fan taking five seconds to smoothly reach maximum RPM is psychoacoustically much less disruptive to the human ear than a fan hitting maximum RPM in half a second, even though the final decibel level is identical.

3. Stabilization Between Benchmark Passes

Many benchmarks, such as 3DMark or Cinebench, feature brief pauses between test scenes or rendering passes. During these pauses, temperatures plummet instantly. A configured step-down delay prevents the fans from immediately dropping to idle, only to loudly spike again three seconds later when the next test starts. The fans maintain a steady, consistent hum across the entire benchmarking session.

Thermal Trade-offs During Heavy Loads

While increasing the fan smoothing delay improves acoustics, setting the delay too high can negatively impact benchmark scores.

If a step-up delay is set to an extreme duration (e.g., 10 seconds or more), the processor may reach its thermal throttling limit before the fans receive the command to spin at full speed. When the hardware throttles to protect itself, clock speeds drop, resulting in artificially low benchmark scores.

For optimal benchmarking, a step-up delay between 1.5 and 3 seconds and a step-down delay between 3 and 5 seconds generally provides the best balance. This configuration prevents sudden acoustic spikes while ensuring cooling systems react fast enough to prevent thermal throttling during sustained loads.
