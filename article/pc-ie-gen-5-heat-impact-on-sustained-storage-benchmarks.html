PCIe Gen 5 Heat Impact on Sustained Storage Benchmarks

This article explores the direct impact of heat dissipation on the sustained throughput of PCIe Gen 5 storage drives during computer hardware benchmarking. It examines why Gen 5 solid-state drives (SSDs) generate substantial heat, how thermal throttling degrades data transfer speeds, the ways inadequate cooling distorts benchmark scores, and the cooling methods required to achieve accurate, consistent performance metrics.

Why PCIe Gen 5 Drives Run Hot

PCIe Gen 5 SSDs double the theoretical bandwidth of Gen 4, reaching sequential read and write speeds of up to 14,000 MB/s. Achieving these speeds requires multi-channel controllers running at higher clock speeds and denser, faster NAND flash memory. Consequently, power consumption has increased, with many Gen 5 drives drawing 10 to 14 watts under full load. Because all of this power is concentrated into a compact M.2 2280 form factor, the electrical energy converts rapidly into dense pockets of heat.

The Mechanics of Thermal Throttling

To prevent permanent silicon damage, NVMe SSDs utilize built-in thermal sensors to monitor the temperatures of the controller and the NAND flash chips. While NAND flash operates efficiently at moderately warm temperatures, the drive's controller becomes unstable if it overheats.

Once a PCIe Gen 5 drive reaches its programmed thermal limit—typically between 70°C and 85°C—a firmware safety mechanism called thermal throttling engages. The controller intentionally reduces its operating frequency and voltage. This action immediately lowers heat output, but it does so by drastically cutting the drive's data processing rates.

How Heat Skews Sustained Benchmarks

Hardware storage benchmarks generally measure two scenarios: short bursts of data and sustained workloads. A poorly cooled PCIe Gen 5 SSD might complete a short-burst test before the heat saturates the components, producing an artificially high score.

During sustained throughput benchmarks, the software writes or reads hundreds of gigabytes of continuous data to test the drive's endurance and stability. Under these conditions, heat accumulates within seconds. If heat dissipation is inadequate, thermal throttling triggers early in the test. Instead of measuring the maximum sustained capability of the storage hardware, the benchmark measures the drive's throttled safety state. In severe cases of poor heat dissipation, a throttled Gen 5 SSD will drop to transfer speeds slower than a PCIe Gen 3 drive,or it may shut down entirely to protect itself, resulting in a failed benchmark run.

Separating Cache Exhaustion from Thermal Limits

When benchmarking sustained write speeds, all modern SSDs experience a natural performance drop once their fast pSLC (pseudo-Single-Level Cell) cache fills up, forcing the drive to write directly to slower TLC or QLC NAND.

Poor heat dissipation makes it difficult to analyze this behavior. If a drive thermally throttles during a sustained write test, a hardware tester cannot easily determine if a sudden drop in throughput is caused by the drive running out of cache or by the controller overheating. Effective heat dissipation eliminates the thermal variable, ensuring that the benchmark accurately reflects the true architectural limits of the controller and NAND flash.

Cooling Requirements for Accurate Testing

Maintaining peak sustained throughput during benchmarks requires a cooling solution that can dissipate heat faster than the drive generates it.

  • Bare Drives: Benchmarking a PCIe Gen 5 SSD without a heatsink is unviable. The drive will hit its thermal limit in seconds, sometimes even while idling or booting the operating system.
  • Passive Heatsinks: Thick aluminum or copper heatsinks, including those integrated into modern motherboards, are the minimum requirement. They rely on thermal pads to transfer heat away from the controller and into the metal fins.
  • System Airflow: Passive heatsinks fail during long benchmarks if the air around them is stagnant. Consistent case airflow is required to move heat away from the M.2 slot.
  • Active Cooling: For open-air test benches that lack case fans, or for prolonged stress testing, active heatsinks featuring built-in micro-fans or vapor chambers are necessary. These solutions force air directly over the fins, ensuring the PCIe Gen 5 drive never reaches its throttling threshold during the benchmark.
