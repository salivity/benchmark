How Flow-Through GPU Coolers Affect CPU Air Cooler Temps

This article provides an overview of how dual-axial flow-through graphics card coolers impact the thermal performance of CPU air coolers during hardware benchmarking. While the flow-through GPU design significantly improves graphics card thermals by allowing air to pass unimpeded through the heatsink fins, it exhausts a concentrated plume of hot air directly into the intake path of standard tower-style CPU air coolers. Consequently, during combined CPU and GPU benchmarking or heavy gaming workloads, CPU temperatures rise due to the elevated ambient temperature of the air entering the CPU heatsink, which can ultimately influence processor boost clocks and benchmark scores.

The Mechanics of Flow-Through GPU Coolers

Traditional open-air GPU coolers feature a printed circuit board (PCB) that runs the entire length of the card. When the fans push air into the heatsink, the solid PCB blocks the air from going straight through, forcing the heated exhaust out the sides of the card—mostly against the motherboard and the side panel of the PC case.

Modern dual-axial flow-through designs utilize a much shorter PCB while keeping the heatsink long. This leaves the rear section of the heatsink completely exposed. The fan on the far right of the GPU pushes air directly through the exposed heatsink fins and upwards toward the top of the case. This dramatically reduces thermal resistance for the GPU, keeping it cooler and quieter.

The Clash with CPU Air Cooler Intake

In a standard ATX computer case, a tower-style CPU air cooler sits directly above the primary PCIe slot where the GPU is installed. Standard case airflow moves from the front of the case to the back. A CPU air cooler relies on pulling in cool air coming from the front intake fans and pushing it through its own heatsink toward the rear exhaust fan.

Because the flow-through GPU exhausts hot air vertically right in front of the CPU cooler, the two airflow paths collide. Instead of drawing in cool air from the front of the case, the CPU cooler’s intake fan sucks in the pre-heated exhaust air coming straight out of the graphics card. Since a heatsink's cooling efficiency is directly tied to the temperature of its intake air, feeding it hot air directly raises the temperature of the CPU on a nearly one-to-one basis.

Impact on Hardware Benchmarking

When benchmarking computer hardware, the effect of a flow-through GPU cooler on CPU temperatures depends entirely on the type of test being run.

1. Isolated CPU Benchmarks

In synthetic benchmarks that only stress the processor, such as Cinebench or Prime95, the flow-through GPU design has zero negative impact. Because the GPU is sitting idle, it generates minimal heat. If the GPU fans are spinning, they are simply passing cool case air through the GPU heatsink and into the CPU intake.

2. Isolated GPU Benchmarks

In benchmarks that heavily stress the graphics card with minimal CPU load, such as FurMark or 3DMark Port Royal, the CPU will show noticeably higher temperatures despite low utilization. The GPU is dumping hundreds of watts of heat directly into the CPU cooler, artificially inflating the processor's idle or low-load temperature readings.

3. Combined and Gaming Benchmarks

In tests that load both components simultaneously—such as 3DMark Time Spy, Blender rendering, or actual gaming benchmarks—the thermal impact is at its peak. A high-end GPU can exhaust 300W to 450W of heat. This causes the CPU cooler intake temperature to spike, often resulting in CPU package temperatures that are 3°C to 8°C higher than they would be with a blower-style GPU or an all-in-one (AIO) liquid cooler.

Modern CPUs from Intel and AMD use opportunistic boosting algorithms that scale clock speeds based on available thermal headroom. If the GPU's hot exhaust pushes the CPU closer to its thermal limit (TJMax), the CPU will lower its boost clock speeds. In benchmarking, this translates to slightly lower physics or CPU scores during combined runs compared to isolated runs.

Mitigating the Effect in Testing

To maintain accurate and consistent benchmarking results when using a flow-through GPU and a CPU air cooler, system builders and testers must rely on high-velocity front-to-back case airflow. High-RPM front intake fans can push enough cool air to mix with the hot GPU exhaust, diluting the heat plume before the CPU cooler ingests it. Alternatively, testing on an open-air test bench reduces the heat buildup, as the lack of an enclosure allows the hot GPU exhaust to dissipate into the room rather than being forced strictly through the CPU cooler.
