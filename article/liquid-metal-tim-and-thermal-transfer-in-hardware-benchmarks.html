Liquid Metal TIM and Thermal Transfer in Hardware Benchmarks

This article explores how liquid metal thermal interface material (TIM) revolutionizes heat dissipation during computer hardware benchmarking. By replacing conventional silicone-based thermal pastes with gallium-based liquid metal alloys, enthusiasts and professionals can achieve dramatically higher thermal transfer rates. The following sections break down the science of liquid metal conductivity, its impact on CPU and GPU clock speeds under synthetic loads, and the physical mechanics that allow it to prevent thermal throttling during extreme benchmarking sessions.

The Science of Thermal Conductivity

The primary metric for any thermal interface material is its thermal conductivity, measured in Watts per meter-Kelvin (W/mK). Standard non-conductive thermal pastes, which use silicone or zinc oxide mixed with ceramic, carbon, or aluminum particles, typically offer a thermal conductivity between 4 and 12 W/mK.

Liquid metal is a eutectic alloy consisting mostly of gallium, indium, and tin. Because it is purely metallic, it boasts a thermal conductivity ranging from 70 to 80 W/mK. This massive increase in conductivity drastically lowers the thermal resistance between the heat source and the heatsink, fundamentally changing the speed at which heat can exit the processor.

Mechanics of Heat Transfer

At a microscopic level, the surfaces of a processor and a cooler's baseplate are rough and uneven. These imperfections trap air, which is a terrible conductor of heat. While standard thermal paste fills these gaps, the paste itself becomes a bottleneck when a processor generates sudden, massive heat spikes during a benchmark.

Liquid metal has a much lower viscosity and "wets" the surfaces it touches, filling microscopic valleys much more effectively than thick pastes. This creates a near-seamless metal-to-metal bond. Consequently, the thermal transfer rate becomes nearly instantaneous. Instead of heat pooling in the silicon while waiting to pass through a sluggish paste, the heat dumps straight into the cooler's cold plate the moment the benchmark begins.

Direct-Die Cooling and Delidding

Liquid metal changes thermal transfer rates most noticeably when applied directly to the bare silicon die.

For GPUs, which do not have an integrated heat spreader (IHS), applying liquid metal directly to the graphics die is straightforward and usually drops load temperatures by 5°C to 15°C.

For CPUs, there are two transfer points: from the silicon die to the IHS, and from the IHS to the CPU cooler. Applying liquid metal only on top of the IHS yields minor improvements (1°C to 3°C) because the factory TIM underneath the IHS remains a bottleneck. To maximize thermal transfer, benchmarkers "delid" the CPU—removing the IHS to apply liquid metal directly to the silicon. This modification drastically accelerates heat transfer, routinely dropping CPU temperatures by 10°C to 20°C under synthetic benchmarking loads.

Impact on Benchmarking Performance

Benchmarking software pushes hardware to its absolute limits, generating far more heat than standard gaming or workstation tasks. Modern CPUs and GPUs utilize automated boosting algorithms that dynamically adjust clock speeds based on available thermal headroom.

When using standard paste, a processor might instantly hit its thermal limit (often 90°C to 100°C) during a benchmark and immediately lower its clock speed and voltage to prevent damage—a process known as thermal throttling.

Because liquid metal removes heat from the die at a much faster rate, it keeps the silicon cooler under the same electrical load. This changes benchmarking outcomes in three ways:

  1. Higher Sustained Boost Clocks: The hardware stays below its thermal throttle point, maintaining peak factory boost speeds for the entire duration of the benchmark.
  2. Increased Overclocking Headroom: The 10°C to 20°C temperature drop gives overclockers the thermal budget needed to pump more voltage into the chip, achieving higher manual clock speeds.
  3. Efficiency in Water Cooling: Liquid metal transfers heat so fast that it stops the processor die from overheating before the liquid in a custom water-cooling loop has even had a chance to warm up, utilizing the radiator's capacity much more efficiently.

Material Compatibility and Risks

While liquid metal drastically improves thermal transfer rates, its chemical properties introduce strict limitations.

Gallium, the primary component in liquid metal, destroys aluminum through a chemical reaction that compromises the structural integrity of the metal. Therefore, liquid metal can never be used with aluminum heatsinks. It is compatible with bare copper, though the gallium will slowly alloy with the copper over time, leaving a dry stain that requires reapplication. The ideal surface for liquid metal is nickel-plated copper, which resists gallium absorption and maintains peak thermal transfer rates long-term.

Additionally, liquid metal is 100% electrically conductive. Unlike standard pastes, if a single drop of liquid metal spills onto the exposed capacitors of a CPU or GPU during benchmarking preparation, it will cause a short circuit and permanently destroy the hardware. Users must insulate surrounding components with conformal coating or electrical tape before application.
