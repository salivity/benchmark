Why Reviewers Test Laptop USB-C Charging During Benchmarks

This article explains why hardware reviewers measure USB-C Power Delivery (PD) charging wattage while simultaneously running heavy benchmarks on laptops. By pushing a laptop's processor and graphics card to their maximum limits during charging tests, reviewers can accurately determine the system's peak power draw, identify potential performance throttling, check for battery drain while plugged in, and evaluate the thermal limits of the device's internal power circuitry.

Forcing Maximum Power Draw

When a laptop sits idle on the desktop, it consumes very little electricity. If the battery is already mostly full, the laptop will only trickle-charge, drawing a fraction of what the USB-C charger can actually provide.

Running a hardware benchmark forces the CPU and GPU to operate at 100% capacity. This creates a massive demand for electricity. By running a benchmark while the battery is partially depleted, the laptop is forced to pull power to run the high-end components and charge the battery at the same time. This scenario allows reviewers to see the absolute maximum wattage the laptop's USB-C port is capable of accepting.

Identifying Performance Throttling

Many high-performance laptops, such as gaming rigs and mobile workstations, ship with large, proprietary power bricks that deliver 200W to 330W of power. However, travelers often prefer to leave those heavy bricks at home and use compact 100W or 140W USB-C PD chargers instead.

Because the USB-C charger provides less power than the proprietary brick, the laptop cannot run its hardware at full speed. Reviewers run benchmarks on USB-C power to measure this exact performance drop. Comparing benchmark scores between the standard charger and the USB-C charger shows consumers how much speed they will sacrifice for the convenience of carrying a smaller adapter.

Detecting Battery Drain Under Load

In some laptops, the USB-C port cannot take in enough power to sustain the hardware during heavy tasks. When a reviewer runs a demanding benchmark, the laptop might consume 120W of power while the USB-C port is capped at pulling only 100W.

Testing both simultaneously reveals how the laptop handles this deficit. Reviewers watch the wattage meter and battery percentage to see if the laptop pauses charging to power the components, or if it actually draws from the battery to make up the difference. Knowing if a laptop's battery will slowly die while plugged into a USB-C charger is vital information for professionals who render video or 3D models on the go.

Stress-Testing Thermal Limits and Power Routing

Charging a lithium-ion battery generates heat, and benchmarking computer hardware generates extreme heat. Doing both at the same time puts the ultimate stress on a laptop's cooling system and internal motherboard components, specifically the voltage regulation modules (VRMs) and charging controllers.

If the internal circuitry gets too hot, the laptop will dynamically lower the USB-C charging wattage to prevent damage. By sustaining a benchmark over a long period while monitoring the USB-C wattage, reviewers can see if the laptop suffers from thermal throttling in its charging port. It also proves whether the laptop has "bypass" charging capabilities, which allows the system to route USB-C power directly to the CPU and GPU, bypassing the battery entirely to reduce heat and preserve battery lifespan.
