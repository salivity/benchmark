Why Random 4K Speeds Matter in Storage Benchmarks

Benchmarking computer storage involves much more than measuring how fast a drive can transfer a single, massive movie file. This article explains the critical purpose of testing random 4K read and write speeds in storage benchmarks. It covers what the term "random 4K" actually means, how it differs from the sequential speeds advertised by manufacturers, and why this specific metric is the most accurate indicator of a computer's everyday responsiveness and real-world performance.

What Does "Random 4K" Mean?

To understand the purpose of the benchmark, you must first understand the terminology:

  • 4K (4 Kilobytes): When you format a storage drive (like an SSD or HDD), the file system divides the drive into small chunks called allocation units or clusters. For modern file systems like Windows NTFS, the default size of this chunk is 4 kilobytes. This means the smallest amount of space a file can take up is 4K, and the operating system processes most basic data in 4K blocks.
  • Random: Data is rarely saved in one neat, continuous line. Over time, as files are created, modified, and deleted, those 4K blocks of data get scattered all over the drive. "Random" means the storage drive has to jump to entirely different locations to find or save these 4K blocks.

Sequential vs. Random Speeds

Storage manufacturers heavily advertise sequential read and write speeds on their packaging. Sequential testing measures how fast a drive can read or write a massive file (like a 20GB video) where all the data is lined up neatly in one continuous row. It is the best-case scenario for a drive and produces the highest numbers.

However, your computer rarely performs purely sequential tasks. The vast majority of daily computing involves accessing thousands of tiny files scattered across the drive. Testing random 4K speeds strips away the marketing numbers and reveals how the drive handles the hardest, most common type of workload.

Why Random 4K Read Speeds Matter

Random 4K read performance directly dictates how fast and "snappy" your computer feels during basic use.

When you boot up your operating system, launch a web browser, or load a video game, the computer is not loading one giant file. Instead, it has to locate and read thousands of tiny 4K files—such as system drivers, configuration files, and Dynamic Link Libraries (DLLs)—that are stored in random locations. A drive with high random 4K read speeds will boot up faster, open applications almost instantly, and load game assets without hitching.

Why Random 4K Write Speeds Matter

While you use your computer, the operating system is constantly writing small bits of data in the background without your direct input.

Examples of random 4K writes include:

  • Saving temporary web browser caches as you scroll through websites.
  • Updating operating system logs and registry entries.
  • Autosaving documents you are actively working on.
  • Downloading and installing software updates, which usually consist of thousands of small files.

If a storage drive has poor random 4K write speeds, the drive will become overwhelmed by these background tasks. This creates a bottleneck, causing your system to stutter, freeze, or feel sluggish while multitasking, even if you have a powerful processor and plenty of RAM.

Understanding Queue Depth (QD1 vs. QD32)

When benchmarking random 4K speeds, software usually tests the drive at different "Queue Depths" (QD). Queue Depth refers to how many data requests are waiting in line for the drive to process at the exact same time.

  • 4K QD1 (Queue Depth 1): The benchmark sends one 4K task, waits for it to finish, and then sends the next. This is the most important metric for everyday consumers and gamers. Most daily desktop tasks happen at a low queue depth, making 4K QD1 the ultimate test of a drive's raw latency and real-world responsiveness.
  • 4K QD32 (Queue Depth 32): The benchmark floods the drive with 32 requests at the same time. Consumer PCs rarely hit this level of simultaneous storage traffic. The purpose of high queue depth testing is to find the absolute limit of the drive's controller and to simulate heavy workstation, database, or server workloads.

Summary

The purpose of testing random 4K read and write speeds is to simulate real-world computing. While sequential benchmarks show how fast a drive can move a massive file in an ideal scenario, random 4K benchmarks reveal how fast your operating system will boot, how quickly your apps will launch, and how smoothly your computer will run during everyday multitasking.
