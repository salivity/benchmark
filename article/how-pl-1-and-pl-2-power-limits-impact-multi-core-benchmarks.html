How PL1 and PL2 Power Limits Impact Multi-Core Benchmarks

This article explores how processor power limits, specifically PL1 and PL2, directly control multi-core benchmark scores in modern computer hardware. By dictating how much electrical power a CPU can draw over short bursts and extended periods, these settings determine the maximum clock speeds a processor can maintain under heavy loads. Understanding the relationship between PL1, PL2, and the timing variable known as Tau reveals why identical processors often yield vastly different performance results during intensive multi-core testing.

Understanding PL1, PL2, and Tau

To understand how power dictates benchmark scores, you must first understand the three variables that govern CPU power management. While these specific terms are native to Intel processors, AMD uses a nearly identical system under different names (such as PPT).

  • PL1 (Power Limit 1): This is the long-term, sustained power limit. It usually matches the processor's advertised Thermal Design Power (TDP). It dictates the maximum wattage the CPU is allowed to draw indefinitely.
  • PL2 (Power Limit 2): This is the short-term boost power limit. It allows the CPU to draw significantly more wattage than the PL1 limit to achieve maximum possible clock speeds for brief periods.
  • Tau: This is a time variable, measured in seconds, that dictates how long the CPU is allowed to operate at the high-power PL2 state before it is forced to drop down to the lower PL1 state.

Why Multi-Core Benchmarks Rely on Power Limits

Single-core benchmarks rarely reach a CPU's maximum power threshold because activating only one or two cores requires minimal electricity.

Conversely, multi-core benchmarks push every core and thread on the processor to 100% capacity simultaneously. This demands a massive amount of electrical power. Because a processor's clock speed scales with the power available to it, the wattage ceiling set by PL1 and PL2 directly caps the maximum frequency that all cores can achieve together. More power equals higher clock speeds across all cores, which translates directly to higher benchmark scores.

Short Benchmarks vs. Long Benchmarks

The duration of a multi-core benchmark determines whether PL1 or PL2 dictates the final score.

Short Duration Tests If a multi-core benchmark takes 45 seconds to complete, and the CPU's Tau is set to 56 seconds, the processor will run at its high PL2 wattage for the entire test. Because the test finishes before Tau expires, the PL1 limit is never triggered. The resulting score will be very high, reflecting the CPU's maximum burst capability.

Long Duration Tests In a 10-minute rendering benchmark, the power limits behave differently. The CPU will run at its high PL2 state for the duration of Tau (e.g., 56 seconds) and then immediately drop its power draw down to the PL1 limit for the remaining nine minutes. When the wattage drops, clock speeds across all cores drop significantly. Therefore, PL1 is the primary dictator of scores in long-term benchmarks.

Motherboard Overrides and Score Variations

Users often see vastly different multi-core scores for the exact same CPU model. This happens because motherboard manufacturers frequently ignore the default PL1 and PL2 guidelines set by the CPU manufacturer.

Many mid-range and high-end motherboards set both PL1 and PL2 to "Unlimited" (often programmed as 4096 Watts) by default in the BIOS. When PL1 is raised to match or exceed PL2, the Tau timer becomes irrelevant. The CPU is allowed to draw maximum boost wattage indefinitely. In a 10-minute multi-core benchmark, a system with unlocked power limits will score significantly higher than a system enforcing strict stock limits, even though the processors are identical.

The Thermal Ceiling

Unlocking PL1 and PL2 to their maximum values only increases multi-core scores if the computer's cooling system can handle the resulting heat. Electrical wattage converts directly into thermal energy.

If PL1 and PL2 are completely unrestricted, the CPU will continuously draw maximum power during a multi-core benchmark until it hits its thermal limit (usually 100°C). At this point, thermal throttling overrides the power limits. The processor will forcibly reduce its own power draw and clock speeds to prevent physical damage. Consequently, in a system with unlocked power limits, the CPU cooler becomes the ultimate dictator of the multi-core benchmark score.
