Why CPU Benchmarks Still Use 1080p Low Settings

This article explains why hardware reviewers continue to benchmark high-end CPUs using 1080p resolution at low graphical settings. By intentionally reducing the workload on the graphics card, this testing methodology removes GPU bottlenecks to isolate the processor's true gaming performance. Furthermore, it demonstrates how a CPU will age alongside future graphics card upgrades and provides vital data for competitive esports players aiming for maximum frame rates.

Eliminating the GPU Bottleneck

In any gaming PC, the CPU and GPU work in tandem to produce frames. The CPU calculates game logic, physics, and object positions, then tells the GPU what to render. The GPU then draws the pixels, applies textures, and handles lighting effects.

When you increase the resolution to 1440p or 4K and turn graphics settings to "Ultra," the workload on the GPU increases massively. However, the workload on the CPU remains largely the same regardless of resolution. At 4K, even the fastest graphics card on the market will hit its performance limit long before a high-end CPU breaks a sweat. This is called a GPU bottleneck. If reviewers tested CPUs at 4K Ultra, almost every modern processor would produce the exact same frame rate, because the test would actually be measuring the limits of the graphics card, not the processor.

Isolating the Variable

Good scientific testing requires isolating a single variable. When evaluating a CPU, the CPU itself must be the limiting factor in the system.

By dropping the resolution to 1080p (or even 720p) and setting graphics to low, the rendering workload becomes trivial for a high-end graphics card. The GPU can suddenly render hundreds of frames per second without struggling. This shifts the burden entirely onto the CPU, forcing it to process game logic and feed data to the GPU as fast as its architecture allows. Only under these conditions can reviewers see the actual performance ceiling of a processor and accurately measure the percentage difference between competing chips.

Predicting Future Value

A common criticism of 1080p testing is that nobody buys a top-tier CPU and a flagship GPU just to play at low settings. While true for most casual gamers, 1080p testing serves as a crystal ball for how a CPU will perform in the future.

Graphics card technology advances rapidly. A GPU released three years from now will easily render 4K resolution at the same speeds today's GPUs render 1080p. If a CPU hits a hard limit of 120 frames per second at 1080p today, it will still be limited to 120 frames per second at 4K when paired with a much faster future GPU. Conversely, a CPU capable of pushing 250 frames per second at 1080p today has the headroom to support multiple generations of future graphics card upgrades without holding them back.

Catering to Competitive Esports

There is a specific demographic that genuinely uses high-end hardware at 1080p low settings: competitive and professional gamers. Players of titles like Counter-Strike 2, Valorant, Overwatch 2, and Apex Legends prioritize frame rates and low input latency above visual fidelity.

To fully utilize modern 360Hz, 480Hz, and 540Hz monitors, these players intentionally lower their resolution and graphical settings. For this audience, 1080p low benchmarks are not theoretical stress tests—they are an exact representation of their daily gaming scenarios.
