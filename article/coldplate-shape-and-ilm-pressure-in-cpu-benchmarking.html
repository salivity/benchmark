Coldplate Shape and ILM Pressure in CPU Benchmarking

This article explores the critical mechanical relationship between CPU cooler coldplate geometry and socket Independent Loading Mechanism (ILM) pressures during hardware benchmarking. It covers how ILM forces warp the CPU's Integrated Heat Spreader (IHS), how cooler manufacturers design convex or concave coldplates to counter this warping, and why matching these physical profiles is essential for accurate, repeatable thermal testing.

The Role of the Socket ILM

The Independent Loading Mechanism (ILM) is the retention bracket on a motherboard that locks the CPU into the socket. To ensure every pin makes reliable contact with the CPU pads, the ILM applies immense downward mechanical force.

Because this force is rarely distributed evenly across the entire perimeter of the processor, the CPU substrate and its metal lid—the Integrated Heat Spreader (IHS)—physically bend under the pressure.

  • Intel LGA1700: Uses a rectangular CPU design where the ILM latches down solely on the middle of the two long sides. This concentrated pressure bows the center of the CPU downward, turning a flat IHS into a concave surface.
  • AMD AM5: Uses a squarer package with a much thicker IHS. The ILM pressure here results in far less bending, leaving the IHS relatively flat or slightly convex in the center.

Coldplate Convexity and Concavity

A CPU cooler's coldplate is the metal base that makes direct contact with the CPU's IHS. While intuition suggests that both the IHS and the coldplate should be perfectly flat, cooler manufacturers intentionally machine their coldplates with slight curvatures—either convex (bowed outward) or concave (hollowed inward)—to account for ILM-induced warping.

The goal of any thermal interface is to maximize metal-to-metal contact directly above the CPU's internal silicon dies (the hotspots) while minimizing the thickness of the thermal paste required to fill microscopic air gaps. Thermal paste is much less conductive than copper or nickel; therefore, a thinner layer yields lower temperatures.

How Coldplates and ILMs Interact

When a cooler is mounted, the interaction between the coldplate's geometry and the ILM-warped IHS dictates the thermal transfer efficiency.

Convex Coldplate on a Concave IHS

This is the ideal pairing for sockets with high central bowing, such as Intel's LGA1700. A convex coldplate protrudes slightly in the middle, slotting directly into the concave dip created by the ILM pressure. This creates peak mounting pressure directly over the CPU die, pushing out excess thermal paste and yielding optimal benchmark temperatures.

Flat or Concave Coldplate on a Concave IHS

If a flat or concave coldplate is mounted onto a concave, ILM-warped CPU, the cooler will only make strong contact along the outer edges of the IHS. This leaves a microscopic valley directly over the center of the CPU where the heat is generated. Thermal paste must bridge this wide gap, resulting in poor thermal transfer and artificially high benchmark temperatures.

Convex Coldplate on a Flat or Convex IHS

When a heavily convex cooler is mounted on a processor that does not warp inward under ILM pressure (such as AMD AM5), the contact area is reduced to a tiny pinpoint in the center. The cooler may rock slightly during mounting, leading to uneven pressure over multi-chiplet designs (CCDs) that are located off-center. This causes rapid thermal throttling during heavy benchmarking loads.

The Impact on Hardware Benchmarking

In computer hardware benchmarking, failing to account for coldplate and ILM interactions skews data and invalidates cross-platform conclusions.

  1. Platform-Specific Performance: A cooler with a heavily convex coldplate may top the charts on an Intel test bench because it counters the LGA1700 ILM bend. That exact same cooler may perform poorly on an AMD AM5 test bench because its convex shape misses the off-center compute dies on the flatter AMD IHS.
  2. Contact Frame Variables: Replacing the stock motherboard ILM with an aftermarket contact frame equalizes pressure around the CPU perimeter, eliminating IHS concavity. In benchmarks, adding a contact frame will drastically improve the performance of flat coldplates, while potentially reducing the effectiveness of heavily convex coldplates designed specifically for the stock ILM bend.
  3. Thermal Paste Application: The interaction changes how thermal paste spreads. High central pressure from a convex-to-concave match forces paste outward efficiently. Poor central pressure from mismatched geometries fails to spread the paste, requiring manual spreading by the tester to prevent thermal runaway during stress tests.

To produce accurate hardware benchmarks, testers must evaluate CPU coolers across multiple socket types and explicitly state whether stock ILMs or aftermarket contact frames are used. A cooler is not universally good or bad; its performance is a direct result of how well its coldplate geometry matches the mechanical realities of the socket it is mounted to.
