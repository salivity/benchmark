Measuring Radiator Heat Dissipation in PC Benchmarks

Accurately measuring a radiator's heat dissipation capacity requires isolating the radiator from fluctuating computer hardware variables and testing it under strictly controlled thermal conditions. This article outlines the proper benchmarking methodology, which involves using a synthetic heat load to apply exact wattage, standardizing coolant flow and fan speeds, and calculating the temperature delta (Delta T) between the liquid coolant and the ambient air once the system reaches thermal equilibrium.

1. Use a Synthetic Heat Source

Real CPUs and GPUs fluctuate in power consumption based on workload, silicon quality, and thermal throttling, making them unreliable for strict radiator testing. Instead, use a Thermal Test Vehicle (TTV) or an inline immersion heater connected to a variable DC power supply. This allows you to inject a precise, unwavering amount of heat directly into the cooling loop. Use a calibrated power meter at the power supply output to verify the exact wattage being applied to the liquid.

2. Monitor the Correct Temperatures

To measure the radiator's performance, ignore component die temperatures. You only need to measure the ambient air temperature and the liquid coolant temperature.

  • Ambient Air: Place at least two thermal probes a few inches in front of the radiator's air intake to record the average temperature of the air entering the fins.
  • Coolant: Place calibrated inline liquid temperature sensors at the radiator’s inlet and outlet ports.

Subtract the average ambient air intake temperature from the average liquid temperature. This resulting number is your Delta T (ΔT), which is the foundational metric for radiator thermal performance.

3. Standardize Liquid Flow and Airflow

A radiator's ability to dissipate heat changes drastically depending on how fast the water and air move through it. You must lock these variables across all tests to get accurate data.

  • Coolant Flow Rate: Install a digital flow meter in the loop. Adjust your pump speed until it hits a standardized flow rate, typically 1.0 Gallon Per Minute (GPM) or 150 to 200 Liters Per Hour (LPH). Ensure this flow rate remains constant, as thicker or more restrictive radiators may require higher pump speeds to maintain the same flow.
  • Airflow: Test the radiator using the exact same fans across all benchmarks. Seal the gap between the fans and the radiator with gasket tape to prevent air leakage. Lock the fans to specific RPM steps (e.g., 800 RPM, 1200 RPM, 1800 RPM) using a hardware PWM controller, and verify the speed with a laser tachometer.

4. Reach Thermal Equilibrium

Turn on the heat source, pump, and fans. Allow the system to run until the coolant temperature completely stops rising and flattens out on a graph. This state is called thermal equilibrium—the point where the radiator is dissipating the exact same amount of thermal energy into the room as the heater is putting into the loop. Reaching equilibrium usually takes 30 to 60 minutes. Once reached, log the temperatures for 15 minutes to calculate a stable average.

5. Calculate the Dissipation Capacity

In PC hardware benchmarking, radiator capacity is universally expressed as Watts per 10°C Delta T (W/10ΔT). Because radiator thermal dissipation scales linearly with the temperature delta, you can determine this metric using one of two methods:

  • The Fixed Wattage Method: Apply a constant load (for example, 300W) and wait for equilibrium. Measure the resulting Delta T. Divide your wattage by the Delta T, then multiply by 10. For instance, if a 300W load results in a 12°C Delta T, the math is (300 / 12) * 10 = 250. The radiator's capacity at that specific fan speed is 250W/10ΔT.
  • The Target Delta Method: Apply a heat load and slowly adjust the wattage on your variable power supply until the system stabilizes at an exact 10°C Delta T. Once it holds steady at a 10°C difference between the water and the air, the wattage reading on your power meter is the radiator's exact heat dissipation capacity.
