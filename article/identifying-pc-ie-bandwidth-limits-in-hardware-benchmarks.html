Identifying PCIe Bandwidth Limits in Hardware Benchmarks

This article explores how Peripheral Component Interconnect Express (PCIe) lane bandwidth limitations manifest during computer hardware benchmarking. When high-performance components like graphics cards and NVMe solid-state drives are restricted by insufficient PCIe lanes or older generation slots, benchmarks reveal distinct performance anomalies. Below is a direct breakdown of the primary indicators of PCIe bottlenecks, including capped sequential storage speeds, degraded synthetic bandwidth scores, inconsistent frame times in gaming tests, and delays in productivity workloads.

Dedicated Synthetic Bandwidth Tests

The most direct way PCIe limitations appear is in synthetic tests designed specifically to measure data transfer rates between the CPU and connected components.

  • 3DMark PCI Express Feature Test: This benchmark floods the PCIe bus with data to measure maximum throughput in gigabytes per second (GB/s). A PCIe bandwidth limitation shows up here as a hard ceiling. For example, a PCIe 4.0 x16 card running in a PCIe 3.0 x16 slot will cap out around 12–14 GB/s instead of the expected 24–28 GB/s.
  • AIDA64 GPGPU Benchmark: In this test, the "Memory Copy" metrics—specifically Host-to-Device and Device-to-Host speeds—will show severely reduced throughput compared to the "Device-to-Device" speed, which only measures the GPU's internal memory and remains unaffected by PCIe lanes.

Graphics Card (GPU) Gaming Benchmarks

In gaming benchmarks, PCIe bandwidth rarely restricts peak frame rates unless the limitation is severe, such as running a modern GPU on a PCIe 3.0 x4 connection. Instead, the limitation shows up in data-streaming scenarios and frame pacing metrics.

  • Degraded 1% and 0.1% Low FPS: While average frames per second (FPS) might only drop by 2% to 5% on restricted lanes, the 1% and 0.1% lows will drop drastically. This manifests visually in the benchmark as micro-stuttering when the camera pans quickly and new assets must be loaded over the PCIe bus.
  • VRAM Overflow Penalties: If a benchmark uses more Video RAM (VRAM) than the GPU possesses, the system uses system RAM as overflow, transferring data through the PCIe lanes. If PCIe bandwidth is limited, the benchmark will exhibit catastrophic frame drops, turning into a slideshow.
  • Low GPU Utilization with High CPU Availability: Monitoring software running during a benchmark will show GPU utilization dropping below 95% even when the CPU is not maxed out. The GPU core is left waiting for texture and geometry data to arrive through the narrow PCIe pipeline.

NVMe SSD Storage Benchmarks

Storage benchmarks are the easiest place to spot a PCIe bottleneck, as solid-state drives will hit a literal speed wall dictated by the PCIe generation or lane count.

  • Flatlined Sequential Speeds: In tools like CrystalDiskMark or ATTO Disk Benchmark, a PCIe 4.0 x4 NVMe SSD capable of 7,000 MB/s will hit a hard ceiling of roughly 3,500 MB/s if placed in a PCIe 3.0 x4 slot, or if limited to PCIe 4.0 x2 lanes.
  • Unaffected Random 4K Performance: A hallmark of a PCIe bandwidth limit is that sequential read and write scores are cut in half, while Random 4K Q1T1 (Queue Depth 1, Thread 1) scores remain completely normal. This happens because small, random files do not saturate the maximum bandwidth of the lanes.

Compute, AI, and Rendering Workloads

In workstation benchmarks like Blender, V-Ray, or OctaneBench, the actual rendering phase happens mostly inside the GPU's VRAM. However, PCIe limitations show up at the beginning and end of the tests, or during multi-GPU operations.

  • Extended Scene Load Times: Before the benchmark begins rendering, the scene data must transfer from system RAM to the GPU. Limited PCIe bandwidth results in noticeably longer initialization times before the benchmark timer officially starts, inflating the total time to completion.
  • Poor Multi-GPU Scaling: In benchmarks utilizing multiple GPUs without a dedicated bridge (like NVLink), cards must communicate via the motherboard's PCIe lanes. A bandwidth bottleneck will result in poor scaling, where two GPUs perform only marginally better than one due to communication delays.

Motherboard Chipset Bottlenecks (Simultaneous Benchmarking)

Sometimes PCIe lanes are shared between the motherboard chipset and the CPU. This limitation only reveals itself during stress tests that benchmark multiple components at the same time (such as OCCT or AIDA64 System Stability Tests).

When an NVMe drive connected to the chipset and a secondary GPU or capture card are benchmarked simultaneously, both devices will show fluctuating, unstable transfer graphs. This occurs because they are fighting for the same limited DMI (Direct Media Interface) or chipset uplink bandwidth going back to the processor.
