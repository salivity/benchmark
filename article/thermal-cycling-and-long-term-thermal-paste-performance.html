Thermal Cycling and Long-Term Thermal Paste Performance

This article explores how thermal cycling degradation impacts the long-term performance of thermal paste during computer hardware benchmarking. It explains the physical mechanics of the pump-out effect, material separation, and dry-out caused by repeated heating and cooling. Additionally, it details how these factors skew benchmark results over time by raising temperatures and inducing thermal throttling, and concludes with practical strategies for maintaining consistent thermal performance during long-term testing.

The Mechanics of Thermal Cycling

Benchmarking pushes CPUs and GPUs to their maximum thermal limits, followed by rapid cooling to idle temperatures once the test ends. This repeated process of extreme heating and cooling is known as thermal cycling. As computer hardware heats up during a heavy workload, the microscopic surfaces of the processor die, integrated heat spreader (IHS), and the cooler's cold plate expand and warp slightly. When the benchmark finishes and the hardware cools down, these metals contract back to their original shapes.

How Thermal Paste Degrades

The constant physical movement caused by thermal cycling subjects thermal paste to mechanical stress, leading to three primary forms of degradation:

  • The Pump-Out Effect: As the metal surfaces expand and contract at different rates, they create a microscopic pumping action. Over dozens of thermal cycles, this motion slowly squeezes the thermal paste out from the center of the processor toward the edges. This leaves behind microscopic voids and air gaps in the most critical heat-transfer zones.
  • Phase Separation: Most thermal pastes consist of thermally conductive solid particles (like aluminum oxide or zinc oxide) suspended in a liquid silicone or synthetic oil carrier. Repeated thermal stress causes the liquid carrier to separate from the solid particles, drastically reducing the material's ability to transfer heat evenly.
  • Dry-Out (Baking): Continuous exposure to the peak temperatures generated during stress testing accelerates the evaporation of the paste's liquid carrier. Once the liquid evaporates, the paste becomes dry, brittle, and chalky. Micro-cracks form within the dried paste, trapping air and acting as a thermal insulator rather than a conductor.

Impact on Hardware Benchmarking

For hardware testers and overclockers, degraded thermal paste directly compromises the accuracy, repeatability, and validity of long-term benchmarking data.

  • Premature Thermal Throttling: As air gaps form and the paste dries out, heat cannot transfer efficiently from the processor to the cooler. The hardware reaches its maximum safe operating temperature (TjMax) much faster. To prevent damage, the processor lowers its clock speeds and voltage, resulting in artificially low benchmark scores.
  • Boost Clock Instability: Modern CPUs and GPUs use opportunistic boosting algorithms that rely heavily on thermal headroom. Even a 3°C to 5°C increase caused by degraded paste will reduce the peak frequency and the duration of boost states. This makes the hardware appear slower than it actually is.
  • Corrupted Baseline Data: Long-term benchmarking requires a static test environment to compare different components fairly. If a test bench is used for months without maintenance, the thermal paste degrades incrementally with each test. Hardware tested at the end of a six-month cycle will record worse thermal and acoustic performance than hardware tested when the paste was fresh, invalidating cross-comparisons.
  • Altered Acoustic Profiles: When paste degrades and core temperatures rise, cooling fans must spin faster to compensate. In benchmarks that measure noise-normalized performance, degraded paste will cause a cooler to fail the test or produce inaccurate decibel-to-temperature ratios.

Preventing Benchmark Skew

To maintain data integrity during long-term hardware benchmarking, testers must mitigate the effects of thermal cycling degradation.

  • Use High-Viscosity Pastes: Thicker, high-viscosity thermal pastes are formulated to resist the mechanical squeezing of the pump-out effect. While harder to spread, they remain in place across hundreds of thermal cycles.
  • Switch to Phase-Change Materials: Phase-change thermal pads (such as PTM7950) are solid at room temperature and liquefy only under high heat. When the hardware cools, the material solidifies again, actively preventing pump-out and separation. They become more efficient with thermal cycling rather than degrading.
  • Use Graphene Pads: For test benches where coolers are swapped frequently or run continuously, reusable graphite or graphene thermal pads offer zero degradation over time. Though they may run 1°C to 2°C warmer than top-tier liquid pastes initially, their performance never changes, ensuring 100% consistent benchmark baselines.
  • Enforce a Repasting Schedule: If traditional silicone-based thermal pastes are used, testers must establish a strict maintenance schedule, reapplying fresh paste after a set number of thermal cycles to guarantee consistent thermal conductivity.
