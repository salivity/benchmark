Why Reviewers Use Cost-Per-Frame in Hardware Benchmarks

This article explains why computer hardware reviewers rely on the cost-per-frame metric to determine the true value of graphics cards and processors. By dividing the retail price of a component by its average frame rate across a suite of games, reviewers establish an objective standard for comparing products of different prices and performance tiers. Below, we explore how this calculation works, why it prevents consumers from overpaying, how it exposes diminishing returns, and the few limitations you should keep in mind when using it to make a purchasing decision.

The Basic Calculation

Cost-per-frame is a simple mathematical equation. Reviewers take the current retail price of a hardware component—typically a GPU or CPU—and divide it by the average frames per second (FPS) that the component achieves across multiple gaming benchmarks.

For example, if a graphics card costs $500 and averages 100 FPS across ten tested games at 1440p resolution, its cost-per-frame is $5.00. If another card costs $400 and averages 90 FPS in the same tests, its cost-per-frame is $4.44. The lower the dollar amount, the better the value you are getting for your money.

Cutting Through Marketing Hype

Hardware manufacturers often focus their marketing on raw performance numbers, boasting massive generational leaps without emphasizing price increases. If a new graphics card is 20% faster than the previous generation, it sounds impressive. However, if the manufacturer also raised the price by 20%, the actual value to the consumer has remained completely stagnant.

Cost-per-frame strips away marketing buzzwords and forces a direct correlation between performance and price. It answers the most important question for the average buyer: "How much am I paying for the performance I actually get?"

Exposing Diminishing Returns

In computer hardware, paying twice as much rarely gets you twice the performance. As you move up the product stack from budget to mid-range, and finally to flagship components, the cost-per-frame inevitably rises.

Reviewers use this metric to visually demonstrate where the "sweet spot" lies in the current market. A flagship GPU might offer the highest frame rates in the world, but its cost-per-frame might be double that of a mid-range card. This data helps budget-conscious buyers see exactly where their money stops working efficiently for them.

Adapting to Real-World Pricing

A product's Manufacturer Suggested Retail Price (MSRP) is only relevant on launch day. Over time, hardware prices fluctuate due to supply shortages, retailer discounts, and the release of competing products.

A graphics card that received poor reviews at launch due to a high price might become the best value on the market six months later after a price cut. By recalculating cost-per-frame using current street prices, reviewers can dynamically update their recommendations and compare brand-new hardware against discounted, older-generation parts.

Accounting for Total System Cost

Some reviewers take this metric a step further by calculating the cost-per-frame of an entire PC build rather than just a single component. Buying a cheap GPU seems like a great way to lower your cost-per-frame, but you still have to buy a motherboard, power supply, RAM, and storage to run it. Integrating the baseline cost of the rest of the PC prevents ultra-cheap, low-performing parts from artificially dominating the value charts.

The Limitations of Cost-Per-Frame

While cost-per-frame is the gold standard for measuring raw value, reviewers also note that it does not tell the whole story. Relying solely on this metric ignores several critical factors:

  • Feature Sets: It does not account for proprietary technologies like Nvidia's DLSS, superior ray-tracing capabilities, or better video encoding for streamers.
  • VRAM Capacity: A card might have a great cost-per-frame today, but if it lacks sufficient video memory (VRAM), it will struggle with future games, reducing its long-term value.
  • Power Consumption: A cheaper component that draws massive amounts of electricity will cost you more money on your energy bill over several years, effectively raising its true cost.

Reviewers use cost-per-frame not as the final verdict, but as a foundational baseline. It grounds the review in financial reality before the reviewer weighs in on the subjective features that might justify paying a premium.
