Standardizing Cables and Ports in Monitor Benchmarking

In hardware benchmarking, maintaining strict test consistency is paramount for producing accurate, repeatable results. This article explains why using identical display cables and ports is critical when comparing monitors and testing computer hardware. By standardizing the physical connections between the graphics card and the display, testers eliminate variables related to bandwidth ceilings, signal compression, refresh rate caps, and latency. This ensures that the benchmark measures the actual capabilities of the monitors and hardware rather than the limitations of the connection interface.

Eliminating Bandwidth Bottlenecks

Different generations of HDMI and DisplayPort cables and ports offer vastly different maximum bandwidth capacities. For example, DisplayPort 1.4 provides significantly more bandwidth than HDMI 2.0, while HDMI 2.1 surpasses DisplayPort 1.4.

If you compare two monitors using different port standards or cable ratings, one monitor may be artificially bottlenecked. A lower-tier cable or port can restrict the maximum achievable resolution and refresh rate, leading to false conclusions about a monitor's performance or a graphics card's frame rate output.

Maintaining Consistent Color and Visual Fidelity

When a cable or port cannot handle the raw data required for a specific resolution and refresh rate, the system automatically degrades the image quality to maintain a stable connection. It does this in two ways:

  • Chroma Subsampling: The system compresses color information (shifting from full 4:4:4 RGB to 4:2:2 or 4:2:0), which visibly reduces text clarity and color accuracy.
  • Bit-Depth Reduction: The display signal may drop from 10-bit color to 8-bit color, hindering High Dynamic Range (HDR) performance and causing color banding.

Using identical cables and ports guarantees that both monitors receive the exact same uncompressed signal, allowing for a fair comparison of their panels' true color reproduction.

Accounting for Display Stream Compression (DSC)

To achieve ultra-high refresh rates at 4K or 8K resolutions, modern monitors rely on Display Stream Compression (DSC). However, whether DSC is triggered depends on the native bandwidth of the port being used.

If one monitor is tested over DisplayPort 1.4 (which might require DSC for a specific resolution) and another is tested over HDMI 2.1 (which might handle the same resolution natively without compression), the comparison becomes flawed. Engaging DSC utilizes specific display engine resources on the GPU, which can introduce microscopic variances in latency and alter how the graphics card handles the workload.

Standardizing Variable Refresh Rate (VRR) Behavior

Technologies like NVIDIA G-Sync and AMD FreeSync do not perform identically across all connection types. Historically, G-Sync required DisplayPort to function, while newer iterations support VRR over HDMI 2.1.

Testing one monitor via DisplayPort and another via HDMI can result in different VRR engagement ranges, varying Low Framerate Compensation (LFC) behavior, and inconsistent frame pacing. To accurately measure screen tearing, stuttering, and frame time delivery, the interface protocols must be identical.

Controlling Signal Integrity and Input Lag

Not all cables are manufactured to the same standard. Variations in cable length, shielding, and gauge (thickness) affect signal integrity. A poorly shielded or overly long cable can cause signal degradation, resulting in flickering, black screens, or increased error correction by the GPU, which can subtly impact benchmark scores.

Furthermore, active cables (which use microchips to boost signals over long distances) can introduce slight input lag compared to passive cables. Using the exact same cable—and plugging it into the exact same port on the graphics card—removes physical transit variables when measuring pixel response times and total system latency.
