Why Anechoic Chambers Are Used in PC Hardware Benchmarking

Acoustic testing is a critical component of benchmarking modern computer hardware, as consumers increasingly demand quiet operation alongside high thermal and computing performance. To accurately measure the exact noise levels generated by components like CPU coolers, graphics cards, and power supplies, professional hardware reviewers rely on sound-dampened anechoic chambers. This article explains how these specialized environments eliminate external background noise and stop internal sound reflections, ensuring precise, repeatable, and scientifically valid acoustic data for hardware comparisons.

Eliminating the Ambient Noise Floor

Every standard room has a baseline level of background noise, known as the noise floor. This ambient sound comes from air conditioning, distant traffic, electrical hums, and outdoor activity, typically measuring between 30 and 40 decibels (dBA). However, many modern, high-end computer fans and coolers operate at levels well below 25 dBA.

If a reviewer tests a 20 dBA fan in a 35 dBA room, the microphone will only register the louder room noise, rendering the test useless. A sound-dampened anechoic chamber is heavily insulated to block out external vibrations and sounds. This drops the internal noise floor to between 10 and 15 dBA—quieter than a human breath—allowing microphones to accurately capture the whisper-quiet operation of low-noise PC components.

Stopping Sound Reflections and Echoes

The word "anechoic" literally means "without echo." In a normal room with flat walls, ceilings, and floors, sound waves emitted by a computer fan bounce off these hard surfaces. These reflections overlap with the original sound, artificially amplifying the volume and distorting the audio profile before it reaches the testing microphone.

Anechoic chambers are lined with thick, wedge-shaped acoustic foam or fiberglass panels on every surface. These wedges trap and absorb sound waves rather than reflecting them. By absorbing the echoes, the chamber ensures that the testing microphone only measures the direct sound coming straight from the computer hardware, resulting in an accurate reading of the component's true acoustic output.

Identifying Annoying Frequencies and Coil Whine

Acoustic benchmarking is not just about measuring how loud a component is in decibels; it is also about analyzing how the noise actually sounds to the human ear. Two fans might both operate at 35 dBA, but one might produce a pleasant, low-pitched rush of air, while the other emits an irritating, high-pitched whine.

The pristine environment of an anechoic chamber allows reviewers to perform frequency response analysis. Without room echoes or background hums muddying the audio data, sensitive microphones can isolate specific, undesirable noises. This is essential for detecting issues like motor bearing clicks in liquid cooler pumps or "coil whine"—the high-frequency screeching sound caused by vibrating electrical components on graphics cards and motherboards.

Guaranteeing Scientific Repeatability

The core principle of hardware benchmarking is consistency. To fairly compare a CPU cooler released today against one released two years ago, every single variable in the testing environment must remain identical.

In a standard office or studio, the acoustic environment changes constantly depending on the time of day, weather, and building occupancy. An anechoic chamber provides a static, unchanging environment. By isolating the hardware inside the chamber, reviewers guarantee that their acoustic measurements are 100% repeatable, giving consumers reliable data they can trust when building a quiet PC.
