Liquid Cooler Warm-Up Times for Accurate Benchmarking

Achieving accurate hardware benchmarks with a liquid cooler requires pre-heating the coolant to thermal equilibrium, which typically takes between 15 and 60 minutes depending on the loop size and hardware power output. Without a proper warm-up phase, the liquid simply absorbs the initial heat spike without reflecting the radiator's actual ability to dissipate heat, resulting in artificially low temperature readings. This article explains the exact warm-up durations needed for All-In-One (AIO) and custom liquid coolers, why thermal saturation matters, and how to verify your system is ready for testing.

Recommended Warm-Up Durations

The time required to warm up a liquid cooler depends entirely on the volume of liquid in the system and the wattage of the components heating it.

  • Small AIO Coolers (120mm to 240mm): 15 to 20 minutes.
  • Large AIO Coolers (280mm to 420mm): 20 to 30 minutes.
  • Standard Custom Loops (1 to 2 radiators): 30 to 45 minutes.
  • Large Custom Loops (3+ radiators or external radiators): 45 to 60+ minutes.

Why Liquid Coolers Need a Warm-Up Phase

Air coolers reach their maximum operating temperature within a few minutes because metal heatpipes have a very low thermal capacity. Liquid coolers, however, use water-based coolants with a high specific heat capacity.

When you start a benchmark on a cold system, the coolant absorbs a massive amount of thermal energy before its own temperature rises. If you run a short benchmark—such as a single run of Cinebench or a 5-minute gaming test—on a cold liquid cooler, you are only measuring the cold water's ability to absorb heat. You are not measuring the radiator and fans' ability to exhaust that heat from the system. Warming up the cooler ensures the liquid reaches thermal equilibrium, where the heat entering the loop equals the heat leaving the radiator.

How to Warm Up Your System Properly

To reach thermal saturation efficiently, you must apply a consistent, heavy thermal load to the components inside the cooling loop.

  1. Lock Your Fan and Pump Speeds: Set your fans and pump to the exact speeds or curves you intend to use during the benchmark.
  2. Apply a Synthetic Load: Use a stress-testing utility that generates a steady power draw. For CPUs, use Prime95 (Small FFTs) or Cinebench in a continuous loop. For GPUs, use FurMark or 3DMark Stress Tests. If both components share a custom loop, stress both simultaneously.
  3. Monitor Temperatures: Open hardware monitoring software like HWiNFO64 to track your temperatures in real time.

How to Know When the Cooler is Ready

Time alone is just an estimate; temperature stability is the true indicator of thermal equilibrium.

If your cooler has a built-in coolant temperature sensor, watch the liquid temperature. When the liquid temperature stops rising and remains completely flat (within 0.5°C) for at least 5 consecutive minutes, the cooler is fully warmed up.

If you do not have a coolant sensor, monitor the CPU package temperature or GPU core temperature under a constant wattage load. Once the component's peak temperature plateaus and stops creeping upward for 5 to 10 minutes, the liquid has reached saturation. You can now stop the warm-up stress test and immediately begin your actual benchmarking run.

Factors That Affect Warm-Up Time

Several variables can shorten or extend your warm-up phase:

  • Component Wattage (TDP): A 250-watt CPU will heat up a 360mm AIO much faster than a 65-watt CPU. Low-power components may require an extra 10 minutes to fully saturate a large cooler.
  • Fan Speed: Running fans at 100% speed will cause the system to reach thermal equilibrium faster than running them at a quiet 30% speed, as the maximum temperature ceiling will be lower.
  • Ambient Temperature: Ensure the room temperature is stable. If the room itself heats up during the warm-up phase, your benchmark results will skew higher over time.
