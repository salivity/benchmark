Benchmarking Wi-Fi 7 Network Cards: Speed and Interference

Benchmarking Wi-Fi 7 (802.11be) network interface cards (NICs) requires specialized testing methodologies to evaluate their multi-gigabit speeds and advanced noise-mitigation capabilities. This article provides a straightforward breakdown of how hardware reviewers and engineers set up controlled environments, utilize network traffic generation software, and inject artificial radio frequency noise to accurately measure the real-world throughput and interference resilience of Wi-Fi 7 computer hardware.

The Baseline Testing Environment

Before any data is measured, testers must eliminate external variables. Because standard office or home environments are flooded with unpredictable wireless signals, professional benchmarking takes place inside an RF (Radio Frequency) isolation chamber. This Faraday-like enclosure blocks all outside wireless interference.

The physical testbed requires specific hardware to prevent bottlenecks:

  • The Client PC: A computer equipped with a PCIe 4.0 or 5.0 slot to house the Wi-Fi 7 networking card.
  • The Reference Access Point: A commercial-grade Wi-Fi 7 router.
  • The Server: A secondary computer connected directly to the router via a 10 Gigabit (10GbE) or 25 Gigabit Ethernet port. Because Wi-Fi 7 can easily exceed 2.5 Gbps, standard Ethernet ports will bottleneck the wireless results.

How Throughput Testing Works

Throughput testing measures the maximum amount of actual data (often called "goodput") the network card can transmit and receive over a specific period.

Traffic Generation Testers do not transfer standard files to measure speed, as hard drive read/write speeds can skew the results. Instead, they use command-line tools like iPerf3 or commercial software like Keysight IxChariot. These programs generate synthetic TCP and UDP data packets directly from the system's RAM, blasting them across the wireless link to measure the absolute limit of the network card.

Testing Wi-Fi 7 Specific Features To hit peak throughput, testers configure the network card and router to utilize three core Wi-Fi 7 technologies:

  1. 320 MHz Channels: Testers bind the 6 GHz band to its widest setting (double the width of Wi-Fi 6E) to test maximum data capacity.
  2. 4K-QAM (4096-Quadrature Amplitude Modulation): This packs 20% more data into the same signal compared to Wi-Fi 6. Testers verify this works by placing the card at a very close range to the router.
  3. Multi-Link Operation (MLO): MLO allows a Wi-Fi 7 card to connect to two bands simultaneously (such as 5 GHz and 6 GHz) and combine their speeds. Testers run multi-stream TCP tests to ensure the card's drivers can successfully aggregate both bands into a single, massive data pipe.

Simulating Distance (Attenuation) Instead of walking a desktop PC down a hallway to test range, testers connect the Wi-Fi 7 card’s antenna ports directly to the router using coaxial cables fitted with programmable RF attenuators. Attenuators artificially weaken the wireless signal in precise decibel (dB) steps. By running iPerf3 at 0 dB, 15 dB, 30 dB, and 45 dB of attenuation, testers can accurately graph how the card’s throughput degrades over simulated distances.

How Interference Testing Works

Wi-Fi 7 relies on massive 320 MHz channels, which makes it highly susceptible to frequency overlap from neighboring networks. Interference testing measures how well a network card maintains speed and low latency when the airwaves are congested.

Injecting Controlled Noise Inside the RF chamber, testers introduce a third piece of hardware: an Arbitrary Waveform Generator (AWG) or a secondary group of routers. These devices are programmed to broadcast continuous, competing wireless traffic on the same channels the Wi-Fi 7 card is trying to use.

Benchmarking Preamble Puncturing In older Wi-Fi standards, if interference occurred on a small part of a wide channel, the rest of the channel became unusable. Wi-Fi 7 introduces Preamble Puncturing, which allows the network card to slice out the noisy part of the frequency and keep using the rest.

To benchmark this, testers follow a strict process:

  1. They establish a 320 MHz connection and measure the baseline throughput.
  2. They use a noise generator to blast heavy interference onto a narrow 20 MHz or 40 MHz section inside that 320 MHz channel.
  3. They measure the throughput again. A well-engineered Wi-Fi 7 card will detect the noise, "puncture" (block) that specific 20 MHz sub-channel, and maintain 85% to 90% of its original speed. A poorly optimized card will drop the wide channel entirely, cutting speeds by half or more.

Testing MLO Failover and Latency Interference affects more than just raw download speeds; it ruins connection stability. Testers evaluate MLO's ability to handle interference by flooding the 5 GHz band with noise while leaving the 6 GHz band clean.

During this test, the benchmark software measures jitter (fluctuations in ping) and packet loss. A successful Wi-Fi 7 card will instantly recognize the interference on the 5 GHz band and seamlessly route the data packets over to the clean 6 GHz band in real-time, resulting in zero dropped packets and a flat, stable latency graph.
