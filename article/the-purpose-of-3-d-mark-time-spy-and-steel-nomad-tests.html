The Purpose of 3DMark Time Spy and Steel Nomad Tests

This article provides a comprehensive overview of the 3DMark Time Spy and Steel Nomad benchmarks, detailing their specific roles in evaluating computer hardware. It covers how Time Spy established the standard for DirectX 12 testing, why Steel Nomad was introduced as its modern successor, and how both tools are practically applied by PC enthusiasts and professionals to measure graphics performance, validate overclocks, and test system stability.

The Role of 3DMark Time Spy

Released in 2016, 3DMark Time Spy was designed to measure the gaming performance of PCs using the DirectX 12 API. For nearly a decade, it served as the industry-standard benchmark for non-ray-traced (rasterized) graphics performance.

The primary purposes of Time Spy include:

  • DirectX 12 Evaluation: It tests how well a system utilizes DirectX 12 features, such as asynchronous compute, explicit multi-adapter, and multi-threading.
  • 1440p Gaming Simulation: The standard Time Spy test renders at a 2560 x 1440 resolution, making it an accurate gauge for mid-range to high-end gaming systems of its era. (A "Time Spy Extreme" variant was later added for 4K testing).
  • Whole-System Scoring: Time Spy consists of two graphics tests and one CPU test. The CPU test uses complex physics and simulation workloads to measure processor performance. The final score is a weighted average of both the GPU and CPU results, reflecting overall gaming capability.

The Role of 3DMark Steel Nomad

Launched in 2024, 3DMark Steel Nomad is the official successor to Time Spy. As modern graphics cards became too powerful for Time Spy—often resulting in CPU bottlenecks rather than true GPU evaluations—Steel Nomad was created to push current and future hardware to its limits.

The primary purposes of Steel Nomad include:

  • Heavy Rasterization Testing: Like Time Spy, Steel Nomad does not use ray tracing. Instead, it uses modernized, highly demanding rasterized graphics techniques to stress the raw rendering power of modern GPUs.
  • Native 4K Benchmarking: Steel Nomad runs at a native 3840 x 2160 (4K) resolution by default, reflecting the capabilities of modern graphics cards. A "Steel Nomad Light" version is available for testing lightweight systems, integrated graphics, and mobile devices at 1440p.
  • Pure GPU Focus: Unlike Time Spy, Steel Nomad does not include a dedicated CPU test. It is designed to isolate the graphics card so that the final score is purely reflective of GPU performance, eliminating processor bottlenecks.
  • Cross-API and Cross-Platform Comparisons: While focused on DirectX 12 for Windows, Steel Nomad also supports the Vulkan API and runs across different operating systems, allowing users to compare widely different hardware ecosystems directly.

Practical Applications in Hardware Benchmarking

Both Time Spy and Steel Nomad serve critical functions for PC builders, reviewers, and gamers. They provide a standardized environment to test hardware outside of the variable conditions found in actual video games.

  • Hardware Verification: When building a new PC or installing a new component, running these benchmarks allows users to compare their scores against a global database of identical systems. This verifies that the hardware is performing as expected and is not hindered by bad drivers or incorrect BIOS settings.
  • Overclocking Validation: Users who manually increase the clock speeds of their GPU or CPU use these tests to measure the exact percentage of performance gained. If an overclock is unstable, the demanding nature of the benchmarks will quickly reveal visual artifacts or crash the test.
  • Thermal and Stress Testing: By running Time Spy or Steel Nomad in a continuous loop, users can simulate a worst-case gaming scenario. This helps evaluate whether the computer's cooling system can prevent thermal throttling and ensures the power supply unit (PSU) can handle sustained peak power draw.

While Time Spy remains a valuable tool for benchmarking older or budget-friendly hardware, Steel Nomad is the definitive test for evaluating the raw, non-ray-traced power of modern graphics cards.
