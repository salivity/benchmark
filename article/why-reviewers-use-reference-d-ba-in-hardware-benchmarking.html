Why Reviewers Use Reference dBA in Hardware Benchmarking

Hardware reviewers use reference decibel (dBA) levels, such as 35 dBA or 40 dBA, to create a fair and standardized testing environment for computer components like CPU coolers, PC cases, and graphics cards. This method, known as noise-normalized benchmarking, measures thermal performance at a fixed volume rather than at default or maximum fan speeds. This article explains why standardizing noise levels is critical for accurate hardware comparisons, how it prevents manufacturers from gaming the system with loud fans, and why it best reflects the real-world experience of PC users.

The Flaw in Maximum Speed Testing

When testing cooling hardware out of the box, products operate at wildly different fan speeds. A manufacturer can pair a poorly designed heatsink with an extremely fast, loud fan. In a basic thermal test, this product might achieve low temperatures and appear to be a top performer. However, the reviewer had to endure deafening noise to get those results.

Testing solely at maximum RPM or relying on default factory fan curves does not show which cooler or case is engineered better; it often just shows which manufacturer was willing to include the loudest fan.

Leveling the Playing Field

Noise-normalized testing solves this problem by locking the acoustic output to a specific number, such as 35 dBA. To do this, reviewers place a calibrated sound meter at a fixed distance from the PC and manually adjust the fan speeds until the meter reads the exact target decibel level.

Once every product is generating the exact same amount of noise, the reviewer measures the temperatures. This creates a true apples-to-apples comparison. It shifts the focus away from raw fan speed and tests the actual physical efficiency of the hardware, such as:

  • The quality of a CPU cooler's heat pipes and fin density.
  • The aerodynamic efficiency of the fan blades at lower speeds.
  • The airflow restriction of a PC case's front panel.

Why 35 dBA or 40 dBA?

The A-weighted decibel (dBA) scale measures sound based on how the human ear actually perceives it. Reviewers choose 35 dBA or 40 dBA as their reference points because these numbers represent the "sweet spot" for most consumers.

A quiet bedroom or office typically has an ambient noise floor of around 28 to 32 dBA. When a PC is running at 35 dBA to 40 dBA (usually measured from 30 to 50 centimeters away), it produces a gentle, unobtrusive hum. Above 40 dBA, fan noise becomes distracting, and approaching 50 dBA becomes intolerably loud for daily use without headphones. By choosing 35 dBA or 40 dBA, reviewers are testing hardware at the maximum volume an average user is actually willing to tolerate.

Real-World Relevance for Consumers

Very few people run their computer fans at 100% speed during daily use. Most builders adjust their fan curves in the BIOS to keep their system relatively quiet while gaming or working.

Noise-normalized benchmarking directly translates to this real-world behavior. If Cooler A beats Cooler B by 5 degrees Celsius when both are locked at 35 dBA, the consumer knows for a fact that Cooler A will provide a cooler system without forcing them to compromise on a quiet environment. It removes marketing gimmicks from the equation and highlights the best-engineered products on the market.
