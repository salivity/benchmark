Why 1% and 0.1% Lows Matter in Handheld PC Benchmarks

This article explains why measuring 1% and 0.1% low framerates is essential when benchmarking handheld gaming PCs. While average framerates provide a basic baseline for performance, they often hide severe micro-stutters and frame drops caused by the strict power, thermal, and memory limitations of portable hardware. By focusing on these specific low-end metrics, hardware testers and consumers can accurately evaluate the true smoothness, consistency, and playability of games on handheld devices.

Understanding 1% and 0.1% Lows

When benchmarking a game, software records the time it takes to render every single frame.

  • Average FPS: The total number of frames rendered divided by the total time of the benchmark.
  • 1% Lows: The average framerate of the slowest 1% of frames recorded during the test.
  • 0.1% Lows: The average framerate of the slowest 0.1% of frames recorded.

If a game runs at an average of 60 frames per second (FPS), the 1% and 0.1% lows reveal how badly the framerate dips during the most demanding moments. If the 1% low is 50 FPS, the game is smooth. If the 1% low is 15 FPS, the game suffers from noticeable freezing and stuttering, even though the average remains 60 FPS.

The Unique Bottlenecks of Handheld Hardware

Handheld gaming PCs, such as the Steam Deck, ASUS ROG Ally, and Lenovo Legion Go, do not function like desktop PCs. They rely on Accelerated Processing Units (APUs) that combine the CPU and GPU onto a single chip. This compact design introduces specific bottlenecks that directly cause poor 1% and 0.1% lows.

1. Shared System Memory

Desktop gaming PCs have dedicated Video RAM (VRAM) for the graphics card and separate system RAM for the CPU. Handheld PCs share one pool of memory for both. When a game simultaneously demands heavy CPU data processing and high-resolution texture loading on the GPU, the memory bandwidth maxes out. This traffic jam results in an immediate stutter, which is only captured by 1% and 0.1% low metrics.

2. Strict Power Limits (TDP)

Handhelds operate under strict battery and thermal constraints, usually running between 10W and 30W of Thermal Design Power (TDP). The device must dynamically shift this limited power between the CPU and GPU in real time. If an explosion occurs in-game, the CPU might suddenly draw more power to calculate physics, starving the GPU of power for a fraction of a second. This causes a sharp frame drop that average FPS calculations smooth over, but 0.1% lows expose instantly.

3. Storage Speeds and Asset Streaming

Many users expand their handheld storage using MicroSD cards or budget single-sided NVMe SSDs. When moving quickly through an open-world game, the storage drive must stream assets into the memory on the fly. Slower storage speeds cause the rendering pipeline to pause while waiting for data. These pauses manifest as severe spikes in the 0.1% lows.

Low Base Framerates Magnify Stutter

On a high-end desktop PC running a game at 144 FPS, a sudden 30% drop in performance brings the framerate down to roughly 100 FPS. To the human eye, this drop is barely perceptible.

Handheld gaming PCs rarely achieve high framerates in modern titles; players typically target a locked 30, 40, or 60 FPS. At a baseline of 40 FPS, a 30% drop brings the framerate down to 28 FPS. Because the time between frames is already much longer at lower framerates, any downward spike crosses the threshold where the human brain stops seeing smooth motion and starts seeing a slideshow. Therefore, tight frame pacing—represented by 1% and 0.1% lows being close to the average FPS—is far more important on a handheld than a high average framerate.

Thermal Throttling Detection

Handheld PCs have tiny cooling fans and heatsinks. Over long gaming sessions, heat builds up, forcing the APU to lower its clock speeds to prevent overheating. Often, thermal throttling does not immediately drop the average FPS by a massive margin. Instead, it introduces instability as the chip rapidly bounces off its thermal limit. Tracking 1% and 0.1% lows over an extended benchmark period is the most reliable way to see if a handheld's cooling solution can maintain a stutter-free experience over time.

Conclusion

Relying solely on average FPS when benchmarking handheld gaming PCs gives a false impression of hardware capability. Because portable devices constantly juggle limited power, shared memory, and tight thermal budgets, they are heavily prone to frame pacing issues. Testing 1% and 0.1% lows is the only way to accurately measure these hardware-induced stutters and determine if a game is truly playable on a handheld screen.
