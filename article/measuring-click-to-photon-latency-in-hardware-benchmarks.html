Measuring Click-to-Photon Latency in Hardware Benchmarks

This article provides a complete overview of the methodology used for measuring click-to-photon system latency when benchmarking computer hardware. It breaks down the components of the total system latency pipeline, compares the traditional high-speed camera technique with modern photodiode sensor tools, outlines the step-by-step testing protocol, and explains how to control system variables to ensure accurate, repeatable benchmark data.

Understanding the Latency Pipeline

Click-to-photon latency (also known as end-to-end system latency) is the total time it takes for a physical mouse click to result in a visible change on a display. When benchmarking hardware, you are measuring the sum of three distinct stages:

  1. Peripheral Latency: The time taken for the mouse switch to actuate, the microcontroller to process the signal, and the data to travel via USB to the PC.
  2. PC Latency: The time taken by the OS to register the input, the CPU to process the game logic, the render queue to hand the data off, and the GPU to render the frame.
  3. Display Latency: The time taken for the GPU to transmit the frame over the display cable, the monitor's scaler to process it, and the liquid crystals or OLED pixels to physically change color (pixel response time).

Selecting the Measurement Hardware

There are two valid methodologies for capturing click-to-photon latency: the photodiode sensor method (the modern industry standard) and the high-speed camera method.

Method 1: Photodiode Luminance Sensors (Recommended)

Tools like the NVIDIA Latency Display Analysis Tool (LDAT) or the Open Source Response Time Tool (OSRTT) use a light-sensitive photodiode strapped to the monitor.

  • How it works: The device connects to the PC via USB, acting as a mouse, or physically wires into an existing mouse's switch. When triggered, the tool sends a click command to the PC and simultaneously starts an internal microsecond timer. The timer stops the exact moment the photodiode detects a sharp change in screen luminance (such as a muzzle flash).

Method 2: High-Speed Camera

Before photodiode tools were widely available, reviewers used high-speed cameras capable of recording at 1,000 frames per second (fps) or higher.

  • How it works: An LED is soldered directly to the mechanical switch of a mouse so that it illuminates the exact millisecond the circuit closes. The camera is positioned to capture both the mouse LED and the monitor in the same frame. Latency is calculated manually by counting the recorded frames between the LED lighting up and the monitor displaying the action. At 1,000 fps, each frame equals 1 millisecond of latency.

Step-by-Step Testing Protocol

To accurately benchmark a piece of hardware (such as a new GPU, CPU, or monitor), follow this strict testing procedure:

1. Establish a High-Contrast Visual Cue Configure the test software or game so that a mouse click produces an immediate, stark change from dark to bright. Many modern games include a built-in "Latency Flash Indicator" (often found in NVIDIA Reflex-compatible titles) that draws a solid white box on the screen the moment a click is detected. If unavailable, aim a weapon at a dark wall in-game so the muzzle flash provides the luminance spike.

2. Position the Sensor If using a photodiode sensor, place it directly over the area of the screen where the white box or muzzle flash appears. Because monitors scan out images from top to bottom, the physical placement of the sensor changes the results. Always place the sensor in the exact same spot on the screen for every test.

3. Configure the Luminance Threshold In your sensor's capture software, set the luminance threshold high enough that ambient room light or minor screen fluctuations do not trigger it, but low enough that the initial transition from black to white stops the timer immediately.

4. Automate the Sample Collection System latency fluctuates constantly based on where the click lands within the CPU, GPU, and monitor refresh cycles. A single measurement is useless. Configure the testing tool to automatically fire a click at randomized intervals (to avoid syncing with the monitor's refresh rate) between 0.5 and 1.5 seconds. Capture a minimum of 50 to 100 samples per test run.

Controlling Variables for Hardware Benchmarking

To isolate the performance of the specific hardware you are benchmarking, all other parts of the pipeline must be locked down.

  • Benchmarking GPUs or CPUs: Use the fastest monitor available (e.g., a 240Hz or 360Hz OLED) and a 1000Hz+ gaming mouse. Disable V-Sync, G-Sync, or FreeSync unless specifically testing those features, and leave the framerate uncapped so the CPU and GPU work at their maximum limits.
  • Benchmarking Monitors: Use a top-tier CPU and GPU capable of pushing framerates well beyond the monitor's refresh rate in a lightweight game (like Counter-Strike 2 or Overwatch 2). Keep the mouse and PC settings identical while swapping only the display.
  • Benchmarking Mice: Bypass the tool's internal USB click-generator. Instead, physically wire the latency tool to the mouse's internal switch pins, or use a solenoid rig to physically actuate the mouse button at a consistent speed and force.
  • System Hygiene: Ensure the Windows power plan is set to High Performance, disable background apps, and turn off overlays (like Discord or Steam) that can hook into the rendering pipeline and add latency.

Analyzing the Data

Once the 50 to 100 samples are collected, export the data for analysis.

  1. Remove Outliers: Inspect the dataset for misfires (e.g., 0ms readings caused by screen flicker, or massive spikes caused by a background OS task) and discard them.
  2. Calculate the Mean: Average the remaining samples to get your baseline click-to-photon latency, measured in milliseconds (ms).
  3. Evaluate Consistency: Calculate the standard deviation or chart the minimum and maximum values. A piece of hardware that delivers a 25ms average with a tight variance of +/- 2ms provides a much better user experience than hardware that averages 25ms but fluctuates wildly between 10ms and 45ms.
