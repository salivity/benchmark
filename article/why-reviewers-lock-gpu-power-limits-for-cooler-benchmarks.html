Why Reviewers Lock GPU Power Limits for Cooler Benchmarks

Hardware reviewers lock GPU power limits to reference specifications during benchmarking to create a fair, apples-to-apples comparison of cooler designs. Because aftermarket graphics cards come with varying factory power limits and dynamic boost algorithms, testing them at out-of-the-box settings results in different amounts of heat being generated. By standardizing the power draw to a fixed wattage, reviewers ensure every cooler is tasked with dissipating the exact same thermal load, successfully isolating the physical cooling solution as the sole variable being tested.

Watts Directly Equal Heat

In computer hardware, virtually 100% of the electrical power consumed by a GPU is converted into heat. Therefore, a graphics card drawing 350 watts generates significantly more heat than the exact same GPU core drawing 300 watts.

To scientifically measure how well a heatsink and fan assembly performs, the heat source must remain constant across all tested models. Locking the power limit guarantees that every GPU in the test group produces the exact same thermal output.

Eliminating Factory Overclock Variances

Board partners (such as ASUS, MSI, and Gigabyte) take the same base GPU chip and sell it across multiple product tiers. A base-model card usually adheres to the reference power limit set by NVIDIA or AMD—for example, 250 watts. However, a premium factory-overclocked model might ship with a default power limit of 300 watts to achieve slightly higher gaming performance.

If a reviewer tests these cards at their default settings, the comparison is flawed:

  • The base cooler only has to dissipate 250 watts of heat.
  • The premium cooler has to dissipate 300 watts of heat.

Under these default conditions, the premium card might run just as hot as the base card, misleading consumers into thinking the larger, more expensive cooler is no better than the cheap one. Locking both cards to the 250-watt reference spec forces an equal workload, revealing the true thermal advantage of the larger cooler.

Controlling Dynamic Boost Algorithms

Modern GPUs do not run at fixed clock speeds or voltages. Instead, they use dynamic boosting algorithms that constantly monitor temperature and power draw. If the algorithm detects that the GPU is running cool, it will automatically increase clock speeds and voltage, which increases power consumption and heat.

Without a strict power lock, a superior cooler lowers the GPU temperature, which inadvertently triggers the GPU to draw more power and generate more heat. Capping the power limit to the reference specification prevents the GPU from moving the thermal goalposts during the benchmark.

Pairing Power Locks with Noise Normalization

Locking the power limit is usually combined with noise-normalized testing to get the ultimate measure of cooler efficiency. Just as manufacturers alter power limits, they also use vastly different factory fan curves.

One card might achieve low temperatures simply because its fans are programmed to spin at a loud, aggressive 2,500 RPM, while a competing card runs hotter because it prioritizes silence at 1,200 RPM.

To find out which physical heatsink and fan design is actually better, reviewers do two things:

  1. Lock the GPU power limit so every card generates the same heat.
  2. Lock the fan speeds to a specific noise level (such as 35 dBA) using a sound meter.

When heat generation and fan noise are completely standardized, the graphics card that records the lowest temperature objectively possesses the best-engineered cooler.
