Why Hardware Reviewers Isolate Storage on CPU PCIe Lanes

Hardware reviewers connect solid-state drives (SSDs) directly to the processor's dedicated PCIe lanes during benchmarking to measure the drive's true maximum potential. This article explains how bypassing the motherboard chipset eliminates bandwidth bottlenecks, reduces data latency, and prevents interference from other connected devices, ensuring that benchmark results are entirely accurate and repeatable.

Direct Connection vs. The Chipset Middleman

Motherboards offer two types of PCIe lanes: those connected directly to the CPU and those routed through the motherboard chipset. CPU lanes provide a direct, unshared data highway straight to the processor.

Conversely, the motherboard chipset acts as a traffic hub. Data from a storage drive connected to a chipset lane must first travel to the chipset, get processed, and then pass through a shared connection—known as the Direct Media Interface (DMI) on Intel platforms or the chipset uplink on AMD platforms—to finally reach the CPU.

Eliminating Bandwidth Bottlenecks

The uplink connecting the motherboard chipset to the CPU has a strict bandwidth limit. This single pathway is responsible for carrying data for almost every accessory on the motherboard, including USB ports, SATA drives, Wi-Fi, Ethernet, and onboard audio.

Modern PCIe 4.0 and PCIe 5.0 NVMe SSDs transfer data at extreme speeds that can easily push this shared chipset link to its absolute limit. If a reviewer connects a high-end SSD to the chipset, the benchmark might accidentally measure the ceiling of the motherboard's chipset uplink rather than the maximum speed of the SSD itself. Isolating the drive on dedicated CPU lanes guarantees the SSD has unrestricted bandwidth.

Minimizing Data Latency

Storage benchmarks do not just measure raw transfer speeds; they also measure latency, which is how quickly a drive responds to a data request.

When data has to pass through the chipset before reaching the CPU, that extra physical hop introduces a microscopic delay. While a regular user might never notice this delay in daily tasks, benchmarking software is designed to detect it. Chipset latency visibly lowers benchmark scores, particularly in random read and write tests and Input/Output Operations Per Second (IOPS). Using direct CPU lanes removes the middleman, yielding the lowest possible latency and showing what the drive's controller and flash memory are truly capable of achieving.

Preventing Cross-Component Interference

The golden rule of hardware benchmarking is isolating the component being tested. If a reviewer tests an SSD on chipset lanes, the drive is forced to share resources with the rest of the system.

A sudden spike in network traffic, a high-polling-rate USB mouse, or background audio processing can momentarily consume chipset bandwidth and processing power. This interference causes inconsistent benchmark runs and skewed data. Dedicated CPU lanes completely isolate the storage drive from the rest of the motherboard's routine operations, ensuring that every drive is tested under the exact same, interference-free conditions.

Standardizing Cross-Platform Comparisons

Different motherboards and processor generations feature different chipset designs with varying bandwidth capabilities. However, the direct PCIe specification (such as PCIe 4.0 x4 or PCIe 5.0 x4) connected to the CPU remains heavily standardized. By strictly using CPU lanes, reviewers minimize the motherboard's influence on the test, making it much easier to fairly compare storage benchmark results across entirely different computer systems.
